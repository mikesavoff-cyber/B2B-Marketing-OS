It’s easy to get shiny channel syndrome—we’ve all had it. Whether it’s you, a founder, or a team member saying, “Have you heard of this? Should we try it?” it’s very tempting to jump on the next trend. But do you actually need a new channel? And is it really going to deliver results?

To help answer that, we’re going to use a framework created especially for CXL called the LEAP framework. It helps you think critically about whether and how to test a new channel before you dive in.

In this session, I’m going to show you why you shouldn’t test too many channels at once, and give you a framework to make sure you don’t overdo it. This way, you’ll avoid chasing every shiny opportunity and instead focus on channels that will truly help you grow.

**Balancing Long-Term and Short-Term Channels**

The first principle is about long vs. short-term balance. It’s easy to get caught up in wanting a new channel without asking: is this a long-term or short-term play?

Short-term channels include things like TikTok ads, Snapchat ads, or Meta ads. These can deliver quick boosts, but the results often drop off just as quickly. Long-term channels, like SEO, partnerships, or building a social media presence, take time to build but create sustainable growth.

You need both. Short-term channels can provide immediate results, while long-term channels compound over time. If you only chase quick wins, growth won’t last. But if you only invest in long-term plays, you may not see results soon enough to sustain your business.

As a startup, you’ll likely lean more on short-term experiments because you’re proving concepts, testing messaging, and finding product-market fit. A larger company, however, has the stability to invest more heavily in long-term channels.

So before testing a new channel, check your balance. Are you chasing too many short-term highs, or investing so much in the long term that you’re missing near-term learning and results?

**Evaluating Potential Channels**

Next, evaluate potential channels systematically. There are five things I like to look at:

* Your target audience – Where do they spend time? What jobs are they trying to get done?  
* Competitor and category research – Not just direct competitors, but other brands speaking to the same audience.  
* New channels – They often have lower costs but higher risks. Worth considering, but not blindly prioritizing.  
* Existing channel performance – Look at your current data. What’s working? What isn’t?  
* Linear vs. growth loops – Some channels provide direct results based on spend, while others create compounding effects. Make sure you have a healthy mix.

When prioritizing, I use four key factors:

* Audience fit – Is the channel relevant to your customers?  
* Ability to compete – How hard is it to stand out? Bigger channels are often more competitive.  
* Potential reach – Is the channel big enough to matter?  
* Resource fit – Do you have the budget, skills, and bandwidth to do it well?  
* Your weighting may differ depending on your company’s constraints. For example, if resources are scarce, that factor may carry more weight.

**Assessing a Channel’s Potential**

Once you’ve shortlisted a channel, estimate the potential cost of acquisition. This doesn’t have to be perfect—back-of-the-napkin math is often enough. Use past data, competitor benchmarks, or industry averages to get an idea.

Many brands skip this step and waste effort testing channels that could have been ruled out upfront. Either the costs are too high or the audience is too small. Remember to include not just ad spend, but also time, creative effort, and other hidden costs. Time is money, too.

**Performance Checks**

Finally, when you’re ready to test, structure your experiment. Don’t test too many channels at once. Have a clear plan that includes:

* Overall goal – What are you trying to achieve?  
* Measure of success – What specific outcome defines success (e.g., conversions at a certain CAC)?  
* Duration – How long will you test before making a call?  
* Benchmarks – Intermediate expectations (e.g., after 2 months we expect X, after 3 months Y).  
* Budget and resources – What’s allocated for this test?  
* Backlog of ideas – Different formats or creative angles to try if the first attempt doesn’t work.

One test doesn’t determine whether a channel works for you. Most require iteration and multiple approaches. Having a backlog keeps you motivated and ensures you don’t give up after one failed attempt.

**Wrapping Up**

By following the LEAP framework, you’ll avoid chasing every shiny new channel and instead evaluate opportunities critically. This helps you use resources effectively, balance short-term wins with long-term growth, and run structured experiments that generate real insights.

So, before you leap into testing a new channel—pause, assess, and plan. It could save you a lot of wasted time and money.
