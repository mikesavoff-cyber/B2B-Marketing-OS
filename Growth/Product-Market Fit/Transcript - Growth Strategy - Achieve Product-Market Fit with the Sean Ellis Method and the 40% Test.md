Product market fit surveys are one of the most important things to get a handle on in the early days and even later on as a scale up. You might have product market fit, but going into new markets or new cohorts of users, you might lose it over time. Some people say it's really hard to measure—and it is. That’s why using a lot of different ways is really useful: both qualitative and quantitative metrics.

Quantitative could look like retention. What does your month-two retention look like? How many people are sticking around? Is that enough to ensure you grow in a compound way? Today, though, we're going to look specifically at a qualitative metric, and this is a really common one: the Sean Ellis method for the product market fit survey, which asks people, “How disappointed would you be if our product was no longer around?”

What we're going to go through is how to analyze this and how to make sure you're picking apart the right bits to navigate growth, while ensuring your product roadmap is impactful and prioritized in the right way.

So—you’ve run your survey and you have responses back. Ideally, you want to run this with people who have actually used your product, and you want at least 200 respondents (500 is even better). If you only have 200, once you start slicing the data your sample sizes get quite small.

The first question is: do you have product market fit? You split responses by people who said they would be very disappointed, somewhat disappointed, or not disappointed if your product disappeared. Product market fit is generally defined when at least 40% of respondents say they’d be very disappointed.

The “somewhat disappointed” group are what I’d call “on the fence” users. They’re not your super fans, but they’re also not detractors. The first step in analysis is understanding: why are some people power users and others not? Look at demographic questions and other context from your survey to figure out who you have better product market fit with.

This insight feeds back into marketing and growth: for example, maybe you have a much stronger fit with a particular career group or age bracket. That tells you where to focus acquisition.

If your “very disappointed” score is under 40%, it’s not the end of the world—it just means you need to figure out how to raise it. Improving from 23% to 30%, then 35%, and ultimately hitting 40% is the goal. More than that is exceptional.

To do this, take the people who are “very disappointed” and split them by other survey questions: job type, age, gender, what they were hoping to get out of your product, or how they discovered you. Seeing the differences helps you identify which groups love you more and why.

For example, maybe people in product roles are far more likely to be very disappointed than those in marketing. That tells you something about where your product resonates. You can structure your data in a table or chart—job type, age, traffic source, key feature used—alongside the percentage who would be very disappointed. This lets you see which acquisition channels or demographics bring in stronger users.

Once you have this, you can prioritize your personas. If you have six personas, you might realize only two of them really demonstrate strong product market fit. That focus is crucial.

The next stage is analyzing the “somewhat disappointed” users. This is where you’ll find the gold nuggets for your roadmap. Here’s how to do it:

First, collect responses into a spreadsheet. For each person, note whether they said “very” or “somewhat” disappointed, plus their main benefit or value from the product. The key question here is: “What’s the value you get from our product?”

Copy all of those “main benefit” responses into another tab and create themes. Tools like ChatGPT or Claude can help generate five to ten themes for you, which you can then refine. Once you’ve defined your themes, tag each response with the appropriate theme and count them up.

From here, create a matrix. On one side, you list the “very disappointed” users and their top three value themes. On the other, you analyze the “somewhat disappointed” users—those who recognize the same values but aren’t fully converted. This is crucial: these users understand your product’s value, but something is holding them back. Identifying those blockers gives you a direct roadmap to improve your fit.

For example, maybe your “very disappointed” users say the product’s speed is the best part. Some “somewhat disappointed” users agree it’s fast—but their feedback reveals issues with reliability or integrations. Fixing those issues could convert them into power users.

You repeat this analysis across themes: identify the values, then figure out the top three things holding back the fence-sitters. These become your improvement priorities.

Why is this important? Because your product market fit score is strongly correlated with retention. The more people who say they’d be very disappointed without you, the more likely they are to stick around in month two and beyond. It’s not just a vanity metric—it’s a proxy for retention and a powerful tool for roadmap prioritization.

The trap many teams fall into is over-focusing on requests from power users. They already love your product—they’ll stick around anyway. The real growth lever is converting the “somewhat disappointed” group. That’s where improvements directly move the needle on retention, engagement, and revenue.

In practice, you should run a product market fit survey at least every half year, ideally every quarter. Persistently offering a feedback option in your product settings is also a good idea, but the deep analysis is better done less frequently since it takes time. Tools like Claude for Google Sheets can help automate theme tagging if you have thousands of responses, saving a lot of effort.

In sum: this analysis is one of the most important things you can do for growth. It can feel uncomfortable or incomplete, but even a first pass will give you insights that sharpen your acquisition, improve funnel conversion, and increase lifetime value.
