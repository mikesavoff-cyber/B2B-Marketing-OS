For any startup, product market fit is the most important thing. And if you just run the survey and look at your metrics without talking to your customers, you might not actually know why things are either working or not working—both of which are important to optimize.

If you're a scale-up, you might have entered new markets or gone after new personas. Understanding how your value proposition and product market fit are explained by users can help you optimize. It ensures that as you grow, you enter new markets and customer bases properly.

You might have run the Sean Ellis product market fit survey and thought, “Okay, so what?” You’ve figured out who you have product market fit with, but you still need more detail. Surveys don’t always tell you enough. That’s why you need to dig in with five to ten more users and uncover qualitative insights: why they like you, what else they have going on, and where you fit in the broader competitive landscape.

We’re going to focus on customer interviews. You want to speak to both groups: people who would be very disappointed if your product disappeared, and those who are somewhat disappointed—your fence-sitters. The goal is simple: find out why people love you, and what’s holding others back from loving you.

To keep things clear, go into research with defined questions. For example, with a recent client we focused on two things: messaging and product market fit. For messaging, we asked: what’s our value proposition? Why do we have product market fit? How are we different in a crowded space? Just as importantly—what are we not? Knowing what you’re not helps sharpen clarity.

For product market fit, surveys might reveal why people love you, why others are on the fence, and what they’d like improved. But free-text survey answers are often too shallow to act on. Interviews let you dig into those themes with more detail. Ask targeted questions about value, markets, use cases, and audience overlap. If your product is paid, you also want to understand why people pay—and why they don’t.

Running five interviews with very disappointed users and five with somewhat disappointed users is a strong starting point. Getting good answers to your first two areas (value and improvement opportunities) is essential, and insights on monetization are a bonus.

Before starting, there are some ethical basics: ask for permission to record (it’s almost impossible to analyze well without recordings) and check if they have a hard stop. Then ease in with a light question—“How’s your day going?”—to loosen people up.

Once you begin, think in terms of a timeline. Avoid future-focused questions like, “Would you use this feature?” People often say one thing and do another. Instead, ask about real actions they’ve taken—even if it’s not with your product. For example: “Tell me the last time you paid for a subscription.”

A good structure is:

Start with easy questions: “Tell me about the first time you signed up for the product.”

Move to desired outcome: “What were you hoping to get out of it?”

Then work backwards: “When did you realize this was something you wanted to solve?”

This helps you uncover the timeline of how they discovered, evaluated, and started using solutions. Be patient—ask one question, let them talk, and even embrace a few seconds of silence.

From there, explore their problem-solving journey. What did they try first? What next? What worked or didn’t? If they mention searching online, ask what terms they used and what results came up. Always anchor the conversation in the past, not hypothetical futures.

A key outcome of this research is competitor insight. You’ll discover what alternatives users considered—books, competing products, services like therapy—and what they liked or disliked about each. For example, someone might say books were useful but lacked accountability, while therapy was effective but too expensive. This helps you understand where you sit in the customer’s decision-making process and how you’re differentiated.

Once you’ve mapped this journey, you can clearly position your product. Know how you’re better, where you’re weaker, and how to communicate that difference.

To illustrate, consider a consultant trying to get better at advising clients. They might currently read Wired, TechCrunch, or newsletters. But those sources provide only surface-level, time-limited perspectives. The problem is they’re left with a partial, fragmented understanding. With your product, they could learn to think critically and holistically through deep, structured insights—leading to more confidence, better client outcomes, and recognition as a thought leader.

This is how granular your value proposition needs to be. You need to contextualize the user’s situation, current approach, limitations, desired outcome, and how your product delivers it better. That’s how you get clear messaging for your team, your investors, and your customers.

So, to recap:

Run user interviews to find your competitors.

Map the decision-making process.

Define your value proposition in detail.

Markets are crowded, and options are plentiful. To stand out, you need a deep understanding of why you stand out—then weave that into all your messaging.
