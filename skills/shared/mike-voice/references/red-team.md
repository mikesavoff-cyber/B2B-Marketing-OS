# Red team

Run on every full draft before delivering. Fix what fails, then run it again.

## 1. Lived or studied?

Does it read like someone who lived this problem, or someone who researched it? If it reads
like research, find the scene and rewrite from inside it.

## 2. The swap test

Swap in another marketer's name. If the piece still works, it has no Mike in it. It needs a
stance from the stance bank, a biography fact, or a specific customer scene.

## 3. Claude tells (delete on sight)

- Em-dashes.
- Stock transitions: "Here's what it means in practice." "Here's the thing." "Let's dive in." "This is one of those X that..."
- Stock closers: "That's the whole job." "And that changes everything." "The bottom line:" "In conclusion".
- Bold mini-headers inside a post ("**The real insight:**").
- "It's not X, it's Y" used for rhythm, when neither X nor Y is a specific belief.
- Triads of abstract nouns ("clarity, alignment, and impact").
- Banned words: leverage (as a verb), harness, unlock, seamless, robust, holistic, delve, landscape, elevate, empower, game-changer, "in today's fast-paced".
- Corporate empathy: "We understand that X face challenges."
- Hedges: potentially, could, it appears that, may be warranted, arguably. Mike's one "arguably" is about Sofia, where it's the joke.
- Equal-weight lists where one item clearly matters more.
- A last paragraph that summarizes the piece.

## 3a. Temperature

Mike vents, admits, and laughs at himself. He doesn't lecture. Match the emotional temperature
of the exemplar you pulled.

- At least one line Mike would say out loud, annoyed or laughing, not as a quote card.
- Open from inside the feeling ("it's impossible not to feel anxious"), not from above the
  reader. An opener that talks down to the reader ("If X, you were only doing Y") fails.
- No aphorism closers: "X can do A. It still can't B." or "The best Xs aren't A. They're B."
  End on a turned line, a call to act, or a question.
- Let one stretch run long and messy, the way he writes when he's worked up. Then cut hard.
- If every paragraph is balanced and polished, it's too tidy. Rough one up.

## 3b. One thesis

A post argues one stance, and the others only support it. If a new argument shows up in the
last third, cut it. It's a different post.

## 4. Fingerprint, not parody

- At most one emoji, one vertical-emphasis block, and one "but hey - here we are".
- No more than one joke per short section, and never explained.
- Sentence case, with " - " dashes.

## 5. Collage check

Compare the draft against the notes you read. Count sentences that match a corpus sentence
word for word or nearly (same words, small swaps). More than one fails. Rewrite the extras:
keep the idea and the move, and say it in new words. A draft that reads well only because it
reuses Mike's lines is imitation, not voice.

## 6. Facts

- Every biography fact, number, and name is in the stance bank or the brief Mike gave.
  Anything else becomes `[Mike: ...]`.
- Private biography facts don't go in the draft. Use `[Mike: OK to mention X?]` instead.
- Every quote attributed to someone else came from Mike's brief or a source he gave.

## 7. Register check

- Operator pieces for buyers or executives: no slang, no emojis, and at most one joke.
- Copy: the problem appears before the product, and there's one lead benefit.
- Author: first person, with a stance from the bank.

## 8. Read it out loud

If it sounds like a document, it isn't done.

## Authorship test (for untagged vault notes)

Tag a note `voice-sample: false` if it has two or more of these:

- em-dashes
- stock transitions or closers from section 3
- bold mini-headers inside prose
- evenly balanced paragraphs with no personal detail

Mike's drafts are usually lowercase, use " - ", and have personal stakes and uneven rhythm. When
unsure, tag `false`. A stance can still be taken from it, with a note.
