## The five rings, with the construction example

*Source: Buyer Personas lesson.* The worked example throughout is a construction company owner
evaluating new equipment.

1. **Priority initiatives:** "the driving factors that push someone to buy a solution."
   Example: new equipment helps them grow, replaces old equipment to avoid mechanical issues, and
   meets customer needs they can't meet today.
2. **Success factors:** "the tangible and intangible rewards that a buyer expects." Capture both the
   organizational level and the personal level. Example: confidence that work gets done on time,
   field staff happy with little retraining, and maintenance that is more efficient. Then ask why:
   "we're cutting our budget for maintenance."
3. **Perceived barriers:** "why some buyers wouldn't perceive your company or solution as the best
   option." Example: models are hard to compare, "all manufacturers are alike. There's no
   innovation," and loyalty to an existing brand (procurement wants fewer license agreements).
4. **Buyer's journey:** "the steps they need to go through and the pieces of information they
   need." Example: how easily operators adapt, the specs, financing options, and bulk discounts.
5. **Decision criteria:** "what are the criteria... and why are these things decision criteria?"
   Example: peer recommendations, review sites, and shopping around on price.

## The six steps in detail

*Source: Building Personas lesson.*

**1. Pick the interviewer.** Use a neutral party, never sales. Pick someone "intellectually curious
and is able to kind of dig in, in a tactful way."

**2. Pick who to interview.** Options: existing customers, past customers, pipeline, and people
unaware of you. The last group is favored: "you're actually gathering new insights that will
guaranteed help your business grow." A side benefit: the interview is itself a form of marketing.

**3. Design the questions.**
- Frame them as a story so the buyer tells events "sequentially, how it happened."
- Use a structured, repeatable template, the same set for everyone, "you're not going to ask every
  question to every buyer."
- Map the template to the five rings.
- Two areas to push on:
  - **Decision-making and buying process.** Example: "I need to see an ROI analysis and a demo."
    That tells you what the demo and ROI must show.
  - **Information sources.** Get specifics: which blogs, which LinkedIn pages, which events, what
    they ask friends, and what would convince them.

**4. Conduct the interview.**
- Record it.
- Be curious, empathetic, and respectful.
- Ask open-ended questions.
- Research the person beforehand.
- Tell them it's persona research, since "they might actually become more forthcoming."

**5. Process the findings.**
- Summarize each interview and mark up the transcript.
- Apply the five-rings lens to each interview.
- Pull the quotes.
- "You don't have to boil the ocean."

**6. Present.** Use a buyer profile:
- page 1 covers the basics
- then the top considerations per ring, each mapped to key interview quotes

Example: "Stan, owner and GM of a construction business."

**Tips:**
- 8-10 interviews per persona.
- One profile per buying-committee role.
- Ask why up to three times on key points.
- Avoid yes/no questions.
- Refresh at least every 12 months.
- Run it as a batch first to set the baseline, then keep going with 1-2 interviews a month.
- Deepbench can source interviewees for a fee per interview.

## Information sources, ranked

*Source: Building Personas lesson*, easy to hard:
1. **Internal stakeholders:** sales and customer service, who talk to customers daily.
2. **Secondary research:** analyst and consulting reports, blogs, and articles on buying trends.
3. **Interviews with real buyers:** "the best source of information."

In preliminary mode (arm's-length), add public buyer voice as secondary research:
- review-site text, filtered to 1-3 stars (same technique as `competitor-research`)
- community threads
- case studies
- job posts for the role

## Applying personas (Bringing it All Together)

*Only when asked. Source: Bringing it All Together lesson.*

**Messaging.** "Effective messaging emerges at the intersection of what your buyers want to hear and
what you want to say."
1. Build a **customer needs list** from the rings: priority initiatives, success factors, perceived
   barriers, and decision criteria. De-emphasize the buyer's journey.
2. Build a **capabilities list** separately: features, service, maintenance, satisfaction.
3. **Match them and add proof.** Example: "class-leading load capacity with the ability to lift
   three tons. We recently helped a construction facility increase their capacity from one ton to
   three ton on every machine."
4. Drop needs your capabilities can't meet. Favor capabilities that are differentiated or high-value
   in the buyer's eyes.

**Content.**
- Map content to the buyer's journey.
- Start by making sure there's content that "addresses each perceived barrier."
- Separately, create top-of-funnel thought leadership for buyers not yet ready to buy (Gusto's "Talk
  Shop").
- Course assignment: 3 content pieces for 3 perceived barriers of one persona.

**Product.**
- Compare buyer needs against current capabilities and name the gaps.
- Example: a car buyer wants financing, which inspires a financing arm.
- "This is how product marketing has a say in the product roadmap."
- Course assignment: 3 product gaps.

**Sales enablement.**
- Share insights "as stories, as insights, rather than just a deliverable." Example: CTOs struggle
  to retain engineers, and engineers love the product, so sell that to the CTO.
- Build playbooks: initiatives, perceived hurdles, and how to overcome them.
- Create messaging per persona.
- Write challenge questions per persona: "hard-hitting questions that [buyers] could only answer if
  they had our solution."
