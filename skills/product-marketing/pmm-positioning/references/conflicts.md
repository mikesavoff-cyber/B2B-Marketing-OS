# Conflict register

Where the four schools disagree, and which one wins under what condition. Read before blending
two sources. When Mike rules on a new conflict, add it here in the same format.

Format: the disagreement, what each school says, the ruling, and what flips the ruling.

---

## 1. A statement or a story?

- **Dell (L1):** Drop the positioning statement. "You should use a story rather than a
  positioning statement, because story is more effective and more memorable."
- **MKT1 guide (Step 3):** The answer is a three-blank statement: who, what, why better. Story and brand
  sit in outer layers of the story stack (§5).
- **Dunford (§5, §6):** The output is a canvas. The story appears as the sales pitch, built on
  top of the canvas.

**Ruling:** Both, in order. Stage 3 produces the canvas and statement. Stage 4 builds Dell's
story on top of them. MKT1's hierarchy and Dunford's canvas-then-pitch sequence agree on the
order. Dell's story is the most developed version of the narrative layer.

**Flips when:** Never on order. If Mike wants only a story (for example, a pitch deck with a
fixed comparator already approved), Stage 3 can be a check rather than a full rebuild.

## 2. Who is the comparator: status quo or competitors?

- **Dell (L1, L5, L7):** Compete against the status quo, never competitors. "Unlike other
  solutions" creates negative social proof.
- **Dunford (§3):** Alternatives include both the status quo and competitors that make real
  short lists. Group them by approach.
- **MKT1 guide (Step 2):** The product type sets the comparator. 10x better compares to category
  leaders. New way compares to the old combination of workflows. Vertical compares to the
  horizontal tool. Buy vs. build compares to the internal process.
- **Fletch (§1, §13):** The comparator is a choice. Mature category: other vendors. Immature or
  new: the status quo way of doing the job. Pick two alternatives at early stage.

**Ruling:** MKT1 and Fletch win. The comparator depends on product type and category
maturity. Dunford's list is the input (what customers actually consider). Fletch and MKT1 pick
from that list. Dell's "status quo only" is correct for new way, new paradigm, and immature
categories. It's wrong for a 10x-better product in a mature category, where buyers already
compare vendors.

**Flips when:** A product in an established category chooses to "change the conversation"
(Dell L4, Tenzo Tea vs. coffee in L5). That's a Stage 2 decision to reframe the comparator.
Mike makes it explicitly. It never happens by default.

## 3. When does the category get decided?

- **Dunford (§3, §4):** Category comes last, after alternatives, capabilities, value, and
  best-fit accounts. It's the context that makes the value obvious.
- **MKT1 guide (Step 2):** Product type and comparator come before the statement.
- **Fletch (§1):** The category strategy (mature, immature, new) is the first bet. §14: the
  category *name* comes last.
- **Dell (L4):** Demand type is element 2, decided right after the best customer.

**Ruling:** Split the decision. The category *strategy* (Fletch §1 plus MKT1 product type) is
a Stage 2 hypothesis, because it sets the comparator and awareness level. The category *label*
is finalized in Stage 3, using Dunford's test: does this context make the value obvious to
best-fit accounts? If Stage 3's value themes contradict the Stage 2 strategy, go back to
Stage 2.

**Flips when:** The product already sells with traction in a clear category (Dunford §2's home
turf). Then run Dunford's five steps in order and treat Stage 2 as a check.

## 4. Create a new category or not?

- **Dell (L8):** For category creators, "sometimes you just need to take the leap." Drift
  created conversational marketing.
- **MKT1 (guide, "new way" section):** "Creating a category very rarely makes sense until you
  are a very established company." Use descriptive language. Position against nothing or the
  old way.
- **Fletch (§14, §18):** Anchor on an existing job to be done. Name the category last. Prefer
  an existing category for budget reasons (Snowflake: "the data warehouse built for the
  cloud").

**Ruling:** MKT1 and Fletch win by default. A new category needs a budget path (Fletch §18)
and credibility (Fletch §20) this skill must name. Dell's leap is allowed only if Mike accepts
the Fletch §1 strategy 3 risks in writing in the strategy memo.

**Flips when:** There's no existing budget line and no existing category label that helps
(Fletch §1 strategy 3: "any existing category label would hurt more than help").

## 5. How many differentiators?

- **MKT1 guide (Steps 3, 3.5):** One. "Why it's better has 2-3 differentiators: pick one."
- **Dunford (§3):** 1-3 value themes.
- **Dell (L6):** Superpowers, up to the top five features customers love.

**Ruling:** Different layers, so no real conflict. Statement: one differentiator (MKT1).
Canvas: 1-3 value themes (Dunford). Story: superpowers as the "how," chosen where unique,
loved, and effective overlap (Dell). The statement's one differentiator must be the lead value
theme.

**Flips when:** Never for the statement.

## 6. Does the promise have to be true?

- **Dell (L7):** A simple promise "doesn't need to be true," it needs to "feel true." Slack
  never actually replaced email.
- **Fletch (§20):** Claim only what current credibility supports. "Claiming a position you
  can't back up... trains the market to distrust you."
- **MKT1 guide (Step 3.5):** "Positioning is too aspirational: write a future positioning statement
  separately."

**Ruling:** Fletch and MKT1 win. The simple promise can compress and dramatize a real
transformation that customers report. It cannot promise an outcome the
proof doesn't show. Every simple promise option in Stage 4 names the proof that backs it.

**Flips when:** Never. This is also one of Mike's standing rules.

## 7. Team exercise or solo?

- **Dunford (§2, §4, §9):** Solo positioning "is usually wrong." A cross-functional team is
  required.
- **Fletch (per the Dunford file §9):** More comfortable solo or small group. The Fletch file
  itself says nothing on this.
- **MKT1 (guide):** A PMM quarterbacks it with founders, product, and sales. Warns about "too
  many cooks."
- **Dell (L1):** Bring leadership in. It's a strategy question.

**Ruling:** This skill is structurally solo. It's an agent. So every output is **[Hypothesis]**
until someone who talks to customers confirms it. Stage 1 names who to ask (sales, customer
success, product). Stage 5's red team and Stage 6's sales-pitch test substitute for Dunford's team exercise.

**Flips when:** Mike supplies team input (call notes, a workshop output). Then the relevant
fields can move from hypothesis to confirmed.

## 8. How do we define the best customer?

- **Dell (L2):** Buys fastest, spends most, returns most often, has the most to gain or lose.
  Data: average deal size, time to close, win rate.
- **Dunford (§3, §4):** Best-fit accounts are derived *from* the value themes: who cares most
  about the value only you deliver. Eliminate bad-fit customers first.
- **Repo `icp-definition`:** "Who you deliver the most real business value to... not always who
  closes fastest" (Keyplay).

**Ruling:** Take the ICP from `icp-definition`. Use Dell's three metrics to pick which
customers to interview in Stage 1. Use Dunford's step 4 in Stage 3 as a consistency check:
the best-fit accounts must be the ones who care most about the value themes. A fast-closing
segment that doesn't value the differentiator is not the positioning target.

**Flips when:** There is no ICP output and no data. Then Dell's gut and sales-team methods (L2)
produce a hypothesis best customer.

## 9. Where does the villain come from?

- **Dell (L5):** From customer interviews. The villain is the thing (forms), not the feeling
  (frustration from forms). Common to all customers. Relevant to the demand type.
- **Repo `icp-definition`:** Already writes a Villain field per segment, from M-type energy.
- **Fletch (§14):** The villain is the old way of doing the job to be done.

**Ruling:** Dell's criteria decide. The ICP's villain field is a starting hypothesis. Stage 1
interview evidence confirms or replaces it. The villain must also match the Stage 2
comparator: a villain unrelated to what you're positioned against creates two stories.

**Flips when:** Never on the criteria.

## 10. One statement or one per situation?

*Ruled by Mike after the Skillvue run, 2026-09-27.*

- **MKT1 guide (Step 3):** "You can't write effective positioning for a million audiences." With
  multiple audiences, "choose your primary audience as the focus of positioning. For the remaining
  segments, you typically just need to refine your messaging." With multiple products, "re-run the
  entire process."
- **Fletch §16:** Horizontal products have product-markets-fit: each market needs its own fit.
  §4: every persona needs its own message.
- **Mike:** Stage 3 must deliver positioning statements based on the segments (who) or the
  situations and problems. A core section with no statement fails.

**Ruling:** Both, layered. One core statement for the primary audience (MKT1). Then one
statement per segment or situation, each keeping the same differentiator and category and
changing only the buyer, the situation, and the specific current way it replaces. Copy per
channel and funnel stage stays in `messaging`.

**Flips when:** The product serves a single situation. Then the core statement stands alone.

## 11. How wide should the category frame be?

*Ruled by Mike after the Skillvue run, 2026-09-27.*

- **Dunford §3 component 5:** The category is the context that makes the value obvious to
  best-fit accounts.
- **MKT1 Complete §12, Pocus principle 2:** One landing use case, not a feature list.
- **Mike:** The category can't be one use case when the product informs every decision in a
  domain. Skillvue's data informs hiring, reskilling, promotion, mobility, performance, project
  resourcing, and restructuring. Framing it as "hiring and promotion" or "skills assessment" was
  wrong.

**Ruling:** The category covers the full breadth of what the product decides. The landing use
case lives in the situation statements and the bet, not in the category name. When no existing
label covers the breadth, use an existing category plus the modifier that carries the
differentiator (Fletch §18).

**Flips when:** `icp-definition` narrows the company to one situation on purpose, as a beachhead
(Fletch §16 path 3). Then the category may be the beachhead's, recorded as a deliberate choice.
