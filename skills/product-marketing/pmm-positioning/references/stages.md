# Stages

Load only the stage you're running. Every stage retrieves its methodology through
`retrieval-protocol.md`, using the rows `methodology-map.md` lists for that decision. Everything a
stage produces goes into the working notes; only Stage 3-5 decisions reach the constitution.

## Contents

| Stage | Decision it answers | Checkpoint after |
|---|---|---|
| 1. Evidence ledger | What's true about this company, market, and buyer | none (runs straight into 2) |
| 2. Strategic readings | Which game, comparator, and category strategy | **Checkpoint 1** |
| 3. Core | Category frame, differentiator, statements | none (runs into 4) |
| 4. Story and promise | Change, stakes, villain, promised land, promise options | none (runs into 5) |
| 5. Red team | Do the claims survive attack? | **Checkpoint 2** |
| 6. Test and roll out | Does it land with real buyers? | Approval |

## Stage 1: Evidence ledger

Goal: the evidence base, in three streams (`evidence-ledger.md`). No positioning is written here.

1. **Gate check.** Look for `icp-definition`, `market-segmentation`, and `competitor-research`
   outputs in `competitor-profiles/`, and ask Mike for insider transcripts or call notes.
2. **Readiness.** Dell L1's five questions and Dunford §2. No traction and no sales motion means a
   positioning thesis, not final positioning.
3. **Audience.** From `icp-definition`. Without it, stop and offer to run it. Never pick the
   audience by where the case studies sit.
4. **Situations.** Every decision, problem, or use case the product serves, from the segment map,
   the product's own pages, and insider notes. Group into 3-7. Don't collapse a broad product
   into its most visible use case.
5. **Current ways, per situation.** Use the four groups as a checklist: gut feel, spreadsheets
   and manual processes, competing solutions, outdated alternatives. Then get specific: the
   actual method, who does it, why it breaks for this buyer. If a row would fit another company
   unchanged, rewrite it.
6. **Customer language.** From interviews (Dell L3's 12 questions, 7-10 interviews) or public
   quotes. With no interviews, put Dell's interview guide in the notes and mark customer-language
   rows low confidence. Never simulate an interview.
7. **Critical unknowns.** The ones that block a decision, separated from the ones that can wait.

## Stage 2: Strategic readings

Goal: the bet. This is the largest judgment call in the job.

1. **Classify.** MKT1 product type (guide Step 2), Dell demand type (L4's nine questions), Fletch
   category strategy (§1). Record the `crosswalk.md` row. Disagreements are mismatch flags.
2. **Category strategy.** Test Fletch §1's three strategies against their choose-when conditions.
   Run the category existence test and record the answer. For a new category, answer Fletch §18:
   which existing budget line pays for this?
3. **Comparators.** From the current ways, choose one or two to fight (Fletch §13), guided by the
   product type's natural comparator. Name what you deliberately won't position against, and why.
4. **Awareness.** Problem aware, solution aware, product aware (MKT1 guide Step 3). This sets the
   "what is it" line.
5. **Game.** Stand-out or education (Fletch §19), with what each would cost.
6. **Credibility ceiling.** Fletch §20: what can be claimed with proof that exists today?
7. **Horizontal check.** Many markets: one of Fletch §16's three paths. Default to the beachhead.

**Checkpoint 1, in chat:** audience, situations, the current ways worth fighting, the critical
unknowns, and 2-3 readings. Each reading gets strategy, comparator, game, credibility ceiling,
evidence, and cost. Recommend one. Stop until Mike picks.

## Stage 3: Core

Goal: the answer. Pick each slot's component from the registry in `methodology-map.md` and record
the choice in the notes.

1. **Capabilities to value.** Distinct capabilities against the chosen comparators; ask "so
   what?" until each reaches a business outcome (Dunford steps 2-3). Notes only.
2. **The one differentiator.** The single idea every value theme depends on. Test: remove it and
   see if the other strengths still set the company apart. If not, it's the differentiator and the
   rest are the "how." Write it as one sentence naming the trade-off every current way accepts and
   why only this product escapes it.
3. **Category frame.** Covers everything the product decides (conflict 11) and makes the
   differentiator obvious (Dunford step 5). Test at least three frames: an existing category; an
   existing category plus the modifier that carries the difference (Fletch §18); the company's own
   label. One-line reason for each rejection.
4. **Core statement** for the primary audience (MKT1 guide Step 3): "For [who] who [situation],
   [company] is the [category] that [what]. Unlike [primary current way], it [differentiator], so
   [outcome]." Write the "what" for the Stage 2 awareness level.
5. **Situation statements.** One per situation when buyers, triggers, or current ways differ
   (conflict 10). Same differentiator and category. Proof per row, or "not yet public."
6. **Contrast check.** Every "X, not Y" and "unlike Y" must name a gap a buyer would feel.
   Near-synonyms fail ("evidence, not visibility").
7. **Not for.** Fletch §6's anti-value prop in the notes; the resulting list in the constitution.
8. **Gates, in the notes:** MKT1 guide Step 3.5 signs; name-swap on every statement; specificity
   on every current way; Dunford §9's five checks; Fletch §17; Fletch §20. Fix what fails.

## Stage 4: Story and promise

Goal: a narrative that makes staying with the current way feel risky. Built on the statements;
never replaces them. Scope set by the registry's Story row.

1. **Change and stakes** (Dell L5): a change that already happened; real, relevant, risky; who
   wins and who loses.
2. **Villain** (L5): the thing, not the feeling; common to all best customers; consistent with
   the chosen comparator (conflict 9).
3. **Promised land** (L6): the five elements; what the customer becomes, not the product.
4. **Proof** (L6): three or more points, each tied to the value it proves (Dunford §6).
5. **Simple promise** (L7): 3-5 options, scored on the six elements in the notes, each naming its
   proof (conflict 6), each run through Fletch §8's anti-fluff test.
6. **Emphasis** by demand type (`crosswalk.md`). Check the story against Dunford's pitch setup:
   change → insight, villain → alternative approaches, promised land → perfect world.

## Stage 5: Red team

Run `red-team.md` against Stages 3-4. Revise only where an attack's evidence beats the claim's.

**Checkpoint 2, in chat:** category frame and rejected frames, the differentiator, the core and
situation statements, the story in four lines, the promise options with a recommendation, and the
attacks that changed something. Write the constitution after Mike approves, or as a hypothesis if
he's absent.

## Stage 6: Test and roll out

Goal: find out if it lands. Done means tested and approved.

1. **Pitch storyboard.** Dunford §6's eight components from the statements and story.
2. **Test plan.** Deck in Dell L8's order (promise, change, stakes, promised land, villain,
   superpowers, proof); landing page with the promise on top and proof high and repeated; 5+
   best-fit participants per test; no leading questions; fail criteria written before testing.
   Add Fletch §17's homepage test when a homepage exists.
3. **Results.** Mike or the team runs the tests. Never invent results.
4. **Revise.** Confusion about what it is → Stage 3 "what." Wrong comparison → Stage 2. Flat
   reaction → Stage 4.
5. **Rollout.** Bottom of funnel first, then middle, then top (Dell L8). Share the constitution
   company-wide, with links to the research (MKT1 guide process step 2-3).

A new-category choice (Fletch strategy 3) that tests badly is a recorded risk, not an automatic
kill (Dell L8). After Mike approves the results, bump the constitution's version and set its status
to "approved."
