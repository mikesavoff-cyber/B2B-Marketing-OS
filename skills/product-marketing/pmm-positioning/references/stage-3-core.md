# Stage 3: Positioning core

Goal: produce the answer. The category frame, the one differentiator, the core statement, and
one statement per situation. A new employee, a salesperson, or a homepage writer must be able to
act on these without reading the research.

## Read first

1. Dunford §3 (components 2-5) and §4 (steps 2-5): capabilities, value, best-fit, category.
2. `MKT1 - Complete Marketing Framework.md` §4 Step 3: who, what (by awareness level), why better.
3. `MKT1 - Complete Marketing Framework.md` §4 Step 3.5 and §12: signs the statement isn't
   differentiated, the five mistakes, the Pocus principles.
4. Fletch §3 (differentiation when features match), §13 (choosing alternatives), §14 (anchor on
   the job), §16 (horizontal products), §18 (existing category for budget).
5. Fletch §6: anti-value prop. Dunford §9: quality checks. Fletch §17: homepage test.
6. `conflicts.md` entries 3, 5, 8, 10, 11.

## Do

1. **Capabilities to value.** List distinct capabilities against the Stage 2 comparators. Ask
   "so what?" until each reaches a business outcome (Dunford steps 2-3). This stays in the notes.
2. **The one differentiator.** Find the single idea every value theme depends on. Test: if you
   removed it, would the other strengths still set the company apart? If not, it is the
   differentiator, and the rest are the "how." Write it as one sentence that names the trade-off
   every current way accepts and why only this product escapes it. In the Skillvue run, three
   listed strengths were one idea: verified skill data reliable enough to trust, at the scale of
   the whole workforce, which is what makes AI-driven people decisions trustworthy.
3. **Category frame.** The context that makes the differentiator obvious (Dunford step 5) and
   covers every situation from Stage 1. Test at least three frames:
   - an existing category (budget line, known vendor list);
   - an existing category plus a modifier that carries the difference (Fletch §18, Snowflake);
   - the company's own label, if it has one.

   Reject a frame that fits only one situation, has no budget line, or puts the product on the
   wrong shelf. Record why each was rejected, in one line.
4. **Core statement.** For the primary audience only (MKT1 Step 3):
   "For [who] who [situation], [company] is the [category] that [what]. Unlike [primary current
   way], it [differentiator], so [outcome]."
   The "what" is written for the Stage 2 awareness level. The "unlike" names a specific current way.
5. **Situation statements.** One per situation from Stage 1, same differentiator, a different
   entry: the buyer, the situation, the specific current way it replaces, and the proof. A
   situation with no proof keeps its statement and says "not yet public."
6. **Contrast check.** For every "X, not Y" or "unlike Y," ask whether a buyer would feel the gap.
   Near-synonyms fail ("evidence, not visibility").
7. **Anti-value prop and would-not-position-as.** Fletch §6's four fields in the notes. The
   constitution gets the resulting "not for" and "will not position as" lists.

## Gates (run in the notes, never shown in the constitution)

- MKT1 Complete §4 Step 3.5 signs, each answered.
- Name-swap test on every statement: it fails if a competitor's name fits.
- Specificity test on every current way: it fails if another company could reuse the row.
- Dunford §9: all five quality checks.
- Fletch §17: an argument, a comparison, and a clear "for whom."
- Fletch §20: every claim has current proof.

Fix failures before the checkpoint. Report any you couldn't fix in the chat summary.
