## **1\. Foundation: High-Level Marketing Strategy**

### **Random Acts of Marketing (RAM)**

Check-the-box marketing work — copying tactics from other companies without regard for what will work for your startup. The opposite of impact-focused strategy.

How to avoid it:

* Set a marketing strategy specific to your startup  
* Make plans and goals based on that strategy  
* Connect day-to-day work back to strategy, plan, and goals  
* Use a traffic light system to evaluate projects — double down on greens, let go of reds  
* Don't try to do everything. Stay focused on what works for your startup.

Why it matters: Breakout growth comes from a plan tailored to your company with deliberate, high-upside bets. Not scattered activity. Just because your founder saw a billboard or heard something on a podcast doesn't mean you should drop everything and chase it.

### **Fuel and Engine**

Fuel \= everything you say and create for your audience (content, copy, creative — out loud, in writing, or visually) Engine \= all channels and processes you use to distribute the fuel to your audience

PMM owns fuel strategy. Marketing owns engine strategy. PMM fuels the engine.

Fuel foundation: Story and Perceptions; Positioning Engine foundation: Funnel Mapping and Channel Selection

Core fuel functions: Content & Copy, Community & Events, Comms/PR, Brand & Design Core engine functions: Inbound/Paid/Web, Demand Gen & ABM, Ops & Analytics, Lifecycle & Email Marketing

When making fuel, think about the engine from the start. When choosing engines, ensure you have capacity to create the right fuel. Choose both based on your audience's needs and preferences.

Failure mode: Over-indexing on fuel (creating without distribution) or over-indexing on engine (channels with nothing to say). Or building fuel and engine that don't match each other, or don't match where the audience actually spends time.

### **Marketing Advantages**

Positive dynamics and strengths in a company's business, product, team, or story. When leveraged, they drive faster growth and differentiate marketing strategy from competitors. Catalysts for growth.

Four advantage categories: Product dynamics, Market/Ecosystem, Brand/Content/Community (See full reference table in Section 9\)

It's not an advantage if everyone has it and leverages it the same way. If your advantages are identical to a competitor's — they're table stakes.

Most successful late-stage companies leveraged 2+ Marketing Advantages to get where they are today. If your startup has no clear advantages, adjust product, business strategy, or GTM approach first.

### **Three Drivers of Marketing Strategy**

Three inputs that every marketing strategy must be built from. Cannot copy another company's strategy and expect it to work — no two companies have exactly the same audience, business model, GTM motion, and advantages at the same stage.

Driver 1: Product Marketing Research

* Audience: TAM, ICPs, segmentation  
* Ecosystem dynamics (competitors, complements, frenemies)  
* Positioning

Driver 2: GTM Motion and Business Model

* Sales-led  
* Self-serve and/or product-led  
* Hybrid motion

Driver 3: Marketing Advantages

* Product dynamics  
* Market/ecosystem  
* Brand, content, community

Deliberate ordering: Product marketing research informs GTM motion. Both inform Marketing Advantages. Once you understand the first two drivers, identifying your advantages becomes obvious. Then it's obvious where to focus: on work that accelerates those advantages and leans into your strengths.

### **The 4 Revenue Levers**

The four and only four high-level ways to grow revenue in B2B marketing.

Lever 1 — Expand the Pie (New Markets): Go after a larger portion of market. New verticals, geographies, or audience segments. Go after new markets or test new pies. Create new fuel tailored to the new pie. Test first before going all in.

Lever 2 — Capture More of Same Pie (Current Market): Gain market share in existing market. Continue expanding what's working while adding big-bet tests to jolt growth. Watch for diminishing returns, especially on paid. Don't wait too long to expand from the original pie.

Lever 3 — Capture Pie More Efficiently (New Customers): Increase conversion rates and lower acquisition costs. When top of funnel stays same but conversion rates improve — revenue goes up. Improve web conversion, sign-up or demo flow conversion, and optimize paid.

Lever 4 — Get More Money from Your Slice (Current Customers): Raise prices or drive expansion. Expand revenue per account by driving customers to pay for new features, increase seats, or increase usage. Lifecycle marketing, in-product marketing, and well-executed product launches drive this.

Stack rank these levers every quarter. Get cross-functional buy-in on the ranking before setting goals. Prevents over-indexing on short-term top-of-funnel activity at the expense of efficiency and expansion. Ranking changes quarterly.

## **2\. Team-Wide Planning and Processes**

### **Marketing Decision Dashboard**

A marketing strategy cheat sheet for prioritization. Centralized document summarizing all strategic inputs needed to make daily prioritization decisions.

What it includes:

* Marketing Advantages  
* ICPs in priority order (or grouped by tiers)  
* Perceptions driving fuel strategy  
* Prioritized 4 Revenue Levers driving engine strategy  
* Highest-level team goals

How to use it:

* Reference as you execute day to day  
* Use as a rubric when evaluating campaign and content roadmap decisions  
* Include it as context in GACCS Briefs  
* Share with other teams as a summary of marketing strategy  
* Return to it when prioritization panic sets in

Why it matters: Teams make big strategic decisions during planning — then forget they made them. One place for those decisions speeds up day-to-day prioritization and dramatically improves the ability to pick impact-driving work.

### **Annual Marketing Plan**

A shared summary document outlining how marketing will drive revenue growth this year, set the company up for long-term success, and do so efficiently.

What it includes:

* Marketing strategy overview and vision for the year — how you'll differentiate and win  
* Annual KPO Goals (including non-goals, key dependencies, risks)  
* Bottom-up forecasts for budget and hiring  
* Big-bet projects or campaigns by quarter with goals, audiences, and stakeholders  
* Links to completed strategy exercises and supporting plans

Build it from the Marketing Decision Dashboard inputs. Share broadly. Use it to guide quarterly planning and daily prioritization.

### **KPO Goals (three types — all required)**

KPIs alone are not a marketing plan. Three types of goals are required:

KPIs — Key Performance Indicators: Metrics you plan to hit. From revenue forecasts and ranked Revenue Levers. Core metrics stay consistent over time even as targets shift quarterly.

Projects — Big-bet initiatives: Initiatives that could drive outsized impact. From strategy inputs: what will accelerate your Marketing Advantages, resonate with your audience, reinforce your Perceptions and positioning?

Ops — Behind-the-scenes foundational work: Hiring, new tools, workflow optimization, analytics improvements, building repeatable processes.

Without all three: you end up in linear growth mode — running on a hamster wheel instead of climbing stairs. Marketing covers the entire lifecycle from prospect to customer to evangelist. You can't just set a pipeline number.

### **GACCS Brief (for every meaningful marketing project)**

The connective tissue between strategy, plan, and execution. Create before starting any meaningful marketing project — campaign, content piece, launch, event, anything.

G — Goals: Why are we doing this? What does success look like? OKR, KPI, Why, Non-goals. A — Audience: Audience segment or ICP, funnel stage(s), prospects and/or existing customers. C — Channels (Engine): What's the engine strategy? Growth tactics, paid channels, distribution plan. C — Creative (Fuel): What's the fuel strategy? Unique POV or key message, related storyline/narrative, creative requirements. S — Stakeholders: Who needs to be involved? Who owns it? Who approves it?

Start with a short version to drive alignment and get buy-in. Expand into a full project plan from there. Use the GACCS as a review rubric when stakeholders evaluate work. Use Marketing Decision Dashboard inputs (especially ICPs, Perceptions, Revenue Levers) to fill it out.

Why it matters: Forces answers to questions that prevent wasted work. Without them: teams execute in silos, scope creeps, and work gets done that no one was aligned on.

### **MKT1 Campaign Framework**

Campaigns \= marketing initiatives that combine both fuel and engine. Focused on a specific audience segment, anchored around a theme, for a set amount of time. Product launches, demand gen plays, ABM initiatives, and events are all campaigns.

Five principles for running campaigns:

1. Actually run campaigns — don't work in silos where fuel and engine activities never connect  
2. Plan campaigns in advance; capture biggest campaigns as Project goals  
3. Each campaign needs a single DRI and tight coordination across all of marketing  
4. GACCS Briefs are not busywork — they're a requirement for every campaign  
5. Double down on campaigns that work. Don't keep reinventing the wheel.

The key to step-change growth \= campaigns. If marketing isn't operating effectively, running a big campaign exposes every breakdown immediately.

## **3\. Product and Audience Marketing**

### **Product Marketing Redefined**

PMM is the connective tissue between product, go-to-market, customer teams, and the rest of marketing. It's really audience marketing, not product marketing. PMM goes far beyond knowing your product — it means deeply understanding your audience first, then the market and ecosystem, then your product's place within them.

Three core PMM research inputs:

1. Audience research: Map TAM, define ICPs, understand firmographics and behavior  
2. Market, ecosystem, and competitive research: Where does the product fit; what alternatives and complements exist; which ecosystem partners can accelerate growth  
3. Product knowledge: Features, use cases, roadmap, product benefits

PMM is the hub of marketing. If PMMs only focus on the product, communications fall flat.

### **TAM and ICP Definition**

TAM (Total Addressable Market): The full universe of potential customers — clarity on which company types and roles you can realistically target now and later. Not an analyst-friendly market size number.

ICP (Ideal Customer Profile) — MKT1 always defines at two levels:

* Company level: industry, size, stage, tech stack, firmographics  
* Persona level: the individuals inside those companies — roles, jobs to be done, pain points, behaviors

Many frameworks only define ICP at company level — that's insufficient. You need both.

ICP Prioritization Tiers:

* Core: Proven ICPs driving most of business today  
* Scaling: Successful ICPs you plan to focus on more; small part of business today  
* Testing: Emerging ICPs you're testing  
* Not a priority today: Consciously deprioritized — naming these is as important as naming the ones you target

Rich ICP definition (beyond firmographics) — what to capture for each ICP:

* Goals they need to achieve (jobs to be done, independent of any product)  
* Challenges in workflow (beyond just what your product replaces — all relevant or adjacent workflows)  
* Other tools they use (entire stack \= potential partnership opportunities)  
* Behavior and content consumption: where they find information, how they typically buy, how they like to be communicated with  
* Primary use cases in your product

Why it matters: Creating personas like "Sales Sally" is check-the-box audience analysis. Proper analysis means sizing your TAM, mapping ICPs at both levels, prioritizing them, and using that work to guide all marketing. Without it, you speak to everyone, dilute your message, and erase any advantage over larger competitors.

### **TAM Segment Mapping Template**

For each TAM segment, document:

* Segment name (e.g., "B2B SaaS — Growth Stage")  
* Priority level (Core/Proven, Scaling, Testing, Not a Priority, Future Priority)  
* Industry/vertical  
* Company size range (min and max employees)  
* Geographic scope  
* Revenue range (min and max ARR)  
* Key characteristics (what makes them unique — e.g., proven PMF, has budget, modern SaaS stack)  
* Estimated count (how many companies)

### **Ecosystem Map**

Go beyond competitor list. Three categories:

Competitors: Analyze business model and GTM strategy, positioning, product capabilities, ICPs, size and stage, key customers, homepage hero copy, growth engines, content types. Use to choose the right comparator and positioning, understand differentiators, inform battlecards, and ensure you zig when competitors zag.

Complements: Build list of potential partners. For each: type and size, audience overlap, potential ROI. Include integrations, influencers, communities, affiliates, advisors. Use to drive ecosystem marketing and understand how the audience discovers information, who influences them, and how they buy.

Frenemies: Players between partners and competitors — analysts, media, regulators, platform providers, churned customers, service providers who shape audience thinking (e.g., pricing consultants for a pricing product). Use to understand narratives shaping the audience, anticipate detractors, and gather feedback on product and competitors.

Buyers don't make decisions in a vacuum. They compare you to competitors, alternatives, and tools they already use. They're influenced by analysts, communities, media, thought leaders, and service providers. Startups that only do competitive analysis miss how buyers actually think and choose.

## **4\. MKT1 Positioning Process (Full)**

Positioning is the process of defining how your product solves a problem better than alternatives for your specific audience. It's about comparison. Positioning is NOT the same as messaging or copy — messaging is the application of positioning to various audiences, channels, and funnel stages.

### **The Full 5-Stage Process**

Product Marketing Research → Product Type & Comparator → Positioning Statement → Messaging → Copywriting

* Research: Dive deep. Get the complete picture. Go into the weeds.  
* Product type & comparator: Zoom back out. Synthesize. Pick one direction.  
* Positioning statement: The answer, not the work. Three blanks: who, what, why.  
* Messaging: Get ICP and funnel stage specific. Refine positioning per audience, per stage.  
* Copywriting: Wordsmith for each channel. This is execution, not strategy.

Analogy: Positioning is long division. The research and process is showing the work. The positioning statement is the answer. Too many companies focus so hard on showing the work that they never produce the right answer.

### **Step 1: Product Marketing Research**

Outputs most useful for positioning:

* A prioritized list of audience segments with a clearly defined primary audience — don't be afraid to go narrow  
* A clear mapping of competitors (a 2x2 competitive matrix works)  
* A clear definition of the primary problem the product solves today

If you jump to positioning without completing this, you'll spin your wheels and won't end up in the right place.

### **Step 2: Product Type and Comparator**

Four product types. Each has a natural comparator.

10x Better (in existing category):

* Definition: Better solution in existing category  
* Comparator: Current product leaders in category  
* Problem aware: Yes | Solution aware: Yes  
* Example: Mercury was a 10x better way to bank

New Way (new category):

* Definition: No established solution to problem  
* Comparator: Combo of multiple workflows (tools, services, processes)  
* Problem aware: Rarely | Solution aware: No  
* Example: Slack was a new way to communicate and work

Vertical Solution:

* Definition: Solution for a niche audience  
* Comparator: Horizontal solution not tailored to audience  
* Problem aware: Usually | Solution aware: No  
* Example: Toast was built to manage restaurants

Buy vs. Build:

* Definition: External, productized solution  
* Comparator: Building and managing internal process or tools  
* Problem aware: Usually | Solution aware: Rarely  
* Example: Buy Stripe; don't build your own payments flow

Selection rules:

* Keep primary audience in mind when picking product type  
* If stuck between Vertical Solution and New Way → choose Vertical Solution (leads to more effective positioning)  
* If stuck between Buy vs. Build and New Way → choose Buy vs. Build  
* If no product type fits: either the product isn't differentiated enough, or the audience is too broad — narrow the audience first

The New Way trap: New Way is the most difficult product type. Most companies lose sight of the goal — trying to name the perfect new category or competing against early-stage players in the same space. The correct approach: position against "nothing" or "the old way." The majority of customers come from people not yet using any solution like yours. Early competitors in the same emerging space actually help you — they're helping establish the new way of solving the existing problem.

Product type evolves over time:

* A vertical product often eventually competes in a broader existing category (HubSpot started for marketers, now competes with Salesforce in CRM)  
* A new way eventually becomes a defined category (Asana was a new way to manage work, now part of work management category)  
* A buy vs. build product eventually becomes its own category as external solutions surpass internal ones (Segment → CDP category)

### **Step 3: Write the Positioning Statement**

Before writing: define the core ICP you're focusing on. Cannot write effective positioning for multiple audiences simultaneously.

Who is it for? Requires audience prioritization. Pick the primary audience segment from product marketing research. If you can't clearly choose a product type and comparator, your audience is likely too broad.

What is it? Depends on audience awareness level:

Problem unaware: Remind of problem AND explain solution → "Leave management software that automates the complexities of compliance, claims, and payroll"

Problem aware, not solution aware: Briefly restate problem, clearly explain general solution → "Software that automates leave management"

Problem AND solution aware: Just describe the product clearly, save differentiation for "why better" → "Leave management software"

Why is it better? Laser focus on your comparator. One differentiator. Not a laundry list. If you panic and end up with something meaningless and catch-all like "save time" — that's not the primary differentiator. Other benefits and use cases go in messaging docs.

Positioning statement example (MKT1 meta example for a messaging/copy platform):

* Product type: New way to solve an existing problem  
* Comparator: Using docs and copywriters  
* Who: Product marketers at B2B startups with under $25M in revenue  
* What: A platform for generating messaging and copy for any ICP or channel (audience is problem aware, not solution aware)  
* Why: Write consistent, effective copy — across channels and audiences — faster

### **Positioning Hierarchy**

Product positioning (positioning statement) is NOT your brand story — it's a subset.

Brand Story → Positioning → Messaging architecture:

* Brand Story: Perceptions (storylines) \+ Company mission/vision/story \+ Product story  
* Positioning: Per product (Product 1, Product 2, Product 3\)  
* Messaging: By audience, by funnel stage, by channel

This newsletter focuses on overall product positioning for your primary audience. Once you start adapting positioning to each audience, funnel stage, and channel — that's messaging.

### **Step 3.5: Check That Your Positioning Is Actually Differentiated**

Signs positioning is not clear or differentiated:

* You decided your company needs two product types and can't pick one comparator  
* "Who it's for" is "for any SaaS company" — almost always too broad  
* "What it is" describes every feature ever launched  
* "Why it's better" has 2–3 differentiators — pick one  
* Positioning is too aspirational — write a future positioning statement separately; position for today first  
* You wish you could build a 3D table to explain positioning — it shouldn't be that complicated  
* You could replace your company name with a competitor's and the statement still applies — too many cooks, went with the path everyone felt fine about instead of great about  
* You've written a tagline, not a positioning statement — "Just Do It" is brand positioning, not product positioning

## **5\. Fuel Strategy: Brand, Story, and Content**

### **The Story Stack (Four Nested Layers)**

Product Positioning (innermost) → Product Story → Story → Brand (outermost)

Brand: Everything people think and feel about your company, whether or not they've used your product. Story: A subset of your overall brand — company story (mission, vision, values) plus your product story. Product Story: All the things you say about your product — features, use cases, benefits. Product Positioning: One part of your product story, the most important part.

Perceptions cover your entire story — they span all four layers, from positioning to brand.

The 30% juice rule: Talk about your product ("the juice") only about 30% of the time. The other 70% covers the problems you solve, solutions you enable, market trends, your vision. Don't just sell the juice. Tell the whole story.

### **Perceptions**

The key narratives you want to drive in your market. Written from the audience's perspective. Foundational tenets of your story. Together, they should cover multiple parts of the Story Stack — from company story to product positioning.

How to define them:

* Run a cross-functional exercise with founders, PMM, content, and leaders from product, sales, or CS  
* Write down what you want your audience to believe and repeat back about your company  
* Goal is surfacing raw ingredients, not wordsmithing  
* Marketing then refines and finalizes 3–5 core Perceptions

Three Perception categories:

* Company Insight: What is the core belief that explains why you exist and how you think about the market?  
* Audience or Market: What should your audience believe about the space they operate in?  
* Product: What should your audience believe about your product specifically?

Tips for writing Perceptions:

* Start with: "What do we want people to repeat back about our company?"  
* Write from the customer POV — as if they were saying it (e.g., "DinoCo is the best AI agent for marketers")  
* Limit to 3–5: easy to recall, memorable, uniquely yours. If competitors could claim them, they're not strong enough.  
* Cover the Story Stack: ensure the set spans brand to product

Perceptions vs. Content Pillars:

* Content pillars \= the main topics your company consistently covers — the "chapters" in your book  
* Perceptions \= the storylines and throughlines you want the audience to believe and repeat — they run through all the chapters  
* Pillars organize content. Perceptions define the narrative you want to own.

Perceptions examples:

Stripe (hypothetical, growth-stage, circa 2015):

* Company Insight: Developers should build what's core to their product and buy what's not  
* Audience/Market: Stripe sets the bar for developer docs, community, and support  
* Product: Every product that accepts payments is now using Stripe

OpenAI (hypothetical, at launch, 2022):

* Company Insight: OpenAI sets the pace for modern AI innovation and research  
* Audience/Market: OpenAI tools are quickly becoming part of everyone's work and life  
* Product: OpenAI delivers the most reliable and usable generative AI outputs

Toast (hypothetical, growth-stage, circa 2014):

* Company Insight: Finally, a POS built specifically for restaurants, not generic retail  
* Audience/Market: The most profitable restaurants use Toast  
* Product: Restaurants no longer have to use clunky, expensive hardware or legacy systems

Why Perceptions matter: Growth marketers have KPIs. Content, brand, and PMMs often lack an equivalent north star. Perceptions fill that gap. They align the team around the same differentiated story, prevent defaulting to disconnected messages or feature-only content, and serve as a filter for what's worth creating.

### **Content Is a Product**

Your marketing content is another product you're building for the same audience as your software. It must solve problems, be differentiated, and be managed like product development — with a vision, roadmap, and short and long-term bets.

Three principles:

1. Create content that solves your audience's problems — turn their biggest challenges into content opportunities. If you can't solve it with product, solve it with content.  
2. Create intentionally with a roadmap — not to fill a calendar slot. Build roadmaps factoring in coverage across ICPs, Perceptions, Revenue Levers, and channels.  
3. Experiment with new formats — make shows, not just feeds. Shows are intentional series with consistent voice, recurring themes, and content that builds on itself. Experiment with tools, templates, calculators, quizzes — sidecar products.

### **Strategic Content Roadmap**

Required fields per content item:

* Roadmap title (content concept or working title)  
* Status (Ideas → Scoped Ideas → Drafting → In Review → Editing → Scheduled → Ready to Publish)  
* Content type (Article: How-to/Trends/Opinion/Partner Author; Customer Story; Resource: Data Report/Examples/Calculator; Webinar; etc.)  
* Destination/Channel (Blog, In-Product, Web-Customers, Web-Resources, Social Thread, Press)  
* Gated/CTA/Potential Magnet? (Yes/No)  
* Primary Audience/ICP (Persona 1, Persona 2, Persona 3\)

Build in a structured tool (Asana, Airtable, ClickUp — not Notion). Include all GACCS Brief fields plus: audience, funnel stage, perception, effort, impact. Plan around monthly or quarterly themes based on Perceptions and content pillars. Focus on quality over volume. Repurpose and redistribute proven content before creating new.

### **Mileage: Repurposing and Distribution**

Mileage \= doing more with fuel you've already created and researched.

Two distinct concepts:

* Repurposing: Extends the life of ideas by expanding one strong asset into multiple formats. Multiplies assets.  
* Distribution: Ensures fuel is actually consumed by the audience. Multiplies reach. You need both. Repurposing without distribution \= waste. Distribution without repurposing \= constantly starting from scratch.

How to get mileage:

* Start with audience problem → turn into content idea → develop anchor asset → expand into multiple formats  
* Make bespoke distribution plan for each asset, not one plan for all  
* Keep reusing core Perceptions, concepts, frameworks across articles, video, resources — repetition strengthens story  
* No dead ends: every piece of content should lead somewhere else valuable — another article, a template, an event, a framework  
* Plan repurposing and distribution while creating, not after  
* Keep two roadmaps: one for net-new content, one for revisiting and repurposing old content

### **MKT1 Content Pillars (Example — Emily Kramer / MKT1)**

Three pillar categories that organize all content topics:

B2B Marketing Functions: Growth Marketing, Product Marketing, Ecosystem, Events, Social, Content, Creative, RevOps

Strategy and Process: Org Design, Hiring, GTM Tech, Planning, Campaign Management, Workflows, Cross-Functional

Core "Krameworks" (proprietary frameworks running across everything): No Playbooks, Avoid RAM, Fuel \+ Engine, GACCS Briefs

Coverage principle: Over any 3–6 month period, check for breadth (coverage across all pillar categories) and depth (reinforcement of active perceptions). The overlap between pillars and perceptions \= where to invest the most creative energy.

### **The Kramework Reuse System ("No Dead Ends")**

Introduce in long-form: New frameworks get introduced in dedicated deep-dive newsletters or primary content pieces.

Reuse across everything: That same framework gets referenced in future newsletters, podcast episodes, LinkedIn posts, guest posts, events, speaking slots — each time without re-explaining from scratch.

Build templates: When a framework needs more than explanation — when people need to actually do something with it — build a template or resource. The template is the framework made operational.

No dead ends: Every piece of content leads somewhere valuable next.

Plan repurposing upfront: Every new piece gets a future task to revisit and repurpose.

TV show analogy: Content pillars \= main characters, settings, and small plot lines. Perceptions \= main story arcs. Frameworks \= quirks and catchphrases that keep things consistent. New content \= new episodes, not new shows.

### **Five Content Strategy Guiding Principles**

1. Add value every time: Every piece adds something new — net-new thought, build on someone else's idea, synthesis, thought-provoking question, or genuine entertainment. Don't create to fill a calendar slot.  
2. Stay relevant — know your audience and ecosystem: Watch sales call recordings. Read support feedback. Have direct conversations with prospects and customers. Without this, content becomes irrelevant quickly.  
3. Differentiate: Your content needs to be differentiated just like your product. Start from knowing what your company is uniquely suited to say. Don't let review committees dilute it into something generic.  
4. Be willing to miss: Content is a portfolio strategy. Only a few investments will drive outsized returns. Something can be genuinely valuable to a specific segment even if it doesn't land broadly.  
5. Avoid Random Acts of Marketing: Test one big new thing at a time. Don't chase every trend in your ecosystem. Focus is required to make differentiation sustainable.

## **6\. Engine Strategy: Growth Marketing and Channels**

### **The 6 Growth Engines**

Six high-level buckets that categorize all available marketing channels.

1. Inbound: Content, SEO, thought leadership — pull buyers to you through value  
2. Outbound: Direct outreach — email, LinkedIn, cold calling — push to specific accounts  
3. Product Virality: Growth loops built into the product — referrals, network effects, PLG motions  
4. Events: IRL and virtual — conferences, owned events, field marketing  
5. Ecosystem: Partnerships, integrations, affiliates, communities, co-marketing  
6. Lifecycle: Nurture, onboarding, retention, expansion — marketing to existing contacts and customers

How to choose channels — start from the 3 Drivers:

* Product marketing research: audience workflows, communities, and ecosystem reveal natural channel fits  
* GTM motion: self-serve/PLG leans on inbound and product-led; sales-led leans on outbound, events, ecosystem  
* Marketing Advantages: identify built-in tailwinds and choose channels that accelerate them

Channel testing principles:

* Test first, then scale — avoid trying everything at once  
* Pairing channels is more powerful than using them in isolation  
* Reassess channel mix regularly — channels stop working or need fuel refreshes  
* Turning off one channel can affect performance of others

Two main channel selection mistakes:

1. Copying what everyone else does  
2. Believing the myth that one primary channel is enough

### **Account-Driven GTM**

Structure growth strategy around accounts, not just people. Proactive, not just reactive. Takes advantage of AI and data enrichment.

Step 1: Define TAM and prioritize ICPs. Segment accounts into 3–4 tiers based on fit, value, and intent.

Step 2: Research accounts and contacts using data enrichment tools. Map accounts, add relevant contacts, structure CRM fields.

Step 3: Set up account stages in CRM and marketing automation — not just contact stages. Account stages: Identified → Aware → Engaged → Considering → Opportunity → Closed (auto-update based on intent signals, GTM activity, and contact stage triggers) Contact stages: Identified → Aware → Qualified → Meeting Booked

Step 4: Create playbooks by account tier. Set up always-on campaigns based on tiers, signals, and account stages. Tier 1 gets highest-touch engagement.

Avoid MQL/SQL terminology: Puts team names in front of stages, creates territorialism. Customer journey is never that clean or linear. Use Qualified 1 and Qualified 2 instead.

Why now: Inbound is no longer the default growth driver — search traffic is declining. It's one signal among many. Meanwhile, mapping your full TAM and enriching it with deep data is easier than ever. With tighter budgets, GTM teams are aligning around accounts.

## **7\. Funnel Mapping (Complete)**

### **Why Map the Funnel**

The funnel is not dead — it's an hourglass. The GTM process (getting prospects to your website, to purchase, to keep purchasing, and to tell others) works best when well understood by marketing, sales, and customer success.

SAT-style analogy: Brand Perceptions/Story & Positioning : Content/Fuel Strategy \= Funnel Mapping & Channel Selection : Growth Strategy

### **Three Funnel Maps Every Company Needs**

Map 1: Funnel Flowchart (FigJam or similar visual tool)

* Purpose: Rules of engagement and handoffs between teams  
* What it shows: Who owns each stage, when each team can email prospects, what triggers stage progression  
* Use for: Building marketing automation and CRM flows, training new employees, reference when adding new GTM motions

Map 2: Marketing Activities Inventory (spreadsheet or project management tool)

* Purpose: What is actually happening at each funnel stage  
* What it shows: Personas, messaging, channels, anchor content, rules of engagement  
* Use for: Identifying coverage gaps and overlaps, driving quarterly prioritization

Map 3: Full-Funnel Report (analytics tool or spreadsheet)

* Purpose: Measure what's working, spot and prevent funnel leaks  
* What it shows: Volume, conversion rate, and time in stage — by source — across every stage  
* Use for: Connecting web traffic to revenue, setting conversion-paired KPI goals

### **The Basic Funnel Structure (Hourglass)**

Stages and what to track:

* Aware: \# (volume), % (conversion rate), ⏱ (time in stage)  
* Lead: \#, %, ⏱  
* Qualified 1 (aka MQL): \#, %, ⏱  
* Qualified 2 (aka SQL): \#, %, ⏱  
* Opportunity: $ (dollar value), %, ⏱  
* Closed: \#, %  
* Active / Expanded: $

Loops to design:

* Word of mouth from customers → new prospects re-enter at Aware  
* Invited teammates → skip top of funnel, re-enter at Closed/Active (need custom onboarding drips)

Don't stop at Closed. Track through customers becoming active, expanded, evangelists. The bottom half of the hourglass drives growth as much as the top.

### **Per-Stage Documentation Template**

For each stage document five things:

Definition/Conversion Event: What triggers entry? (e.g., visits website / opens outbound email / fills web form / hits lead score of X / meeting booked / product activity threshold)

Rules of Engagement: Who owns this stage? Who can email at this stage?

Audience and Messaging: Which personas at this stage? What key benefit messaging per persona?

Marketing Activities: Which channels are primary? What anchor content exists here?

High-Level Metrics: Conversion rate and time between stages last month/quarter. Volume in stage. Links to relevant reports.

Goals and Status: Target volume, conversion rate, and time between stages this quarter. OKRs, projects, initiatives. What's working / not working.

### **Sales-Led GTM Funnel Map**

Original Sources: Organic Inbound, Paid Inbound, Referral/Partnerships, Sales Outbound, Automated Outbound (Marketing), Event Registrant

Stage flow: Aware (interacts with content or website, hears about product) → Inbound Lead (visits website and fills out form — note: may already be in CRM) → \[parallel\] Outbound Lead (RevOps & SDRs ensure complete information and remove duplicates — passes from MQL to SQL immediately) → (Marketing) Qualified Lead (segmented outreach drips start) → SQL/Meeting (meeting scheduled — can add SAL stage before SQL) → Opportunity (AE has meeting with prospect, moves through opportunity stages) → Closed (signed deals get onboarded and handed off to CS) → Expanded (upgraded through product or CS) → Evangelist (CS and marketing efforts drive loyalty)

SDR Outreach via 1:1 Email and Phone: For funnel leak intervention and/or enterprise or high-value leads

Lead or Contact Recycling: Nurtured with automated emails at slower cadence

Ongoing Brand Marketing: Brand building, content and education, community — runs across all stages

Ownership by stage:

* Aware → MQL: Marketing \+ SDRs/BDRs for outbound  
* QL → SQL: Sales (support from Marketing)  
* Opportunity → Closed: Sales (support from Marketing)  
* Expanded → Evangelist: CS \+ Marketing (supported by product)

### **Product-Led GTM Funnel Map**

Stage flow: Aware (interacts with content or website, hears about product) → Inbound Lead (visits website and fills out form) → (Marketing) Qualified Lead \[in product\] → Product Qualified Lead/User \[based on pre-defined product activity\] → \[split\] Opportunity: Self-serve Upgrade \[added to nurture sequence\] OR Opportunity: Sales Assist \[sales reaches out to highest-value leads\] → Upgraded/Paid (converted to paid customer) → Expanded (expands usage, contract value increases) → Evangelist (CS and marketing efforts drive loyalty)

Lead or Contact Recycling: Nurtured with automated emails and in-product

Ownership by stage:

* Aware → MQL: Marketing  
* MQL → PQL: Product \+ Marketing  
* PQL → Opportunity: Marketing \+ Sales \+ Product (self-serve) / Sales \+ Product \+ Marketing (sales assist)  
* Closed → Expanded: CS \+ Product \+ Marketing

### **Hybrid GTM Funnel Map**

Two parallel paths that converge:

Outbound/High-value path: Outbound Lead (RevOps & SDRs ensure complete info) → SQL/Meeting (can add SAL stage) → Opportunity: Sales Assist → Closed/Paid

Inbound/PLG path: Aware → Inbound Lead → Free Product (segmented outreach drips start — can call this MQL) → Product Qualified Lead (based on product activity) → Opportunity: Self-serve Upgrade

High-value PQLs escalate from bottom path to Sales Assist.

Both paths feed: Closed/Paid → Expanded → Evangelist

Recycling: Nurtured with automated emails and in-product Ongoing Brand Marketing: Runs across all stages

### **Full-Funnel Reporting Structure**

Track per source per month: Direct Traffic, Organic Search, Paid, Referrals, Social Media, Email, Other

Track by stage:

* Web → Lead: Web Sessions, % of Total Web, Conversion Rate Web→Lead  
* Lead → MQL: Lead volume, % of Total Leads, Conversion Rate Lead→MQL  
* MQL → SQL: Marketing Qualified Leads, % of Total MQLs, Conversion Rate MQL→SQL  
* SQL → Opportunity: Sales Qualified Leads, % of Total SQLs, Conversion Rate SQL→Opportunity

Summary rows: Inbound Total, Outbound Total, Combined Total, Month-over-Month Growth

Attribution approach: Last touch at MQL \+ campaign-influenced reports. First touch alone is misleading — a lead added via a prospecting list that later comes inbound via search will show "list" not "SEO" under first touch.

### **KPI Goal Structure for Funnel Stages**

Never set a volume target without a paired conversion rate target.

Sales-led: "Increase qualified leads to \[X\] while maintaining \[Y\]% conversion rate to opportunity" PLG: "Increase signups to \[X\] while maintaining \[Y\]% conversion rate to activated product user"

If funnel-based marketing goals don't include a paired conversion rate target — reset them now.

### **Fuel and Engine Functions by Layer**

Fuel foundation: Story and Perceptions; Positioning Core fuel functions: Content & Copy, Community & Events, Comms/PR, Brand & Design

Engine foundation: Funnel Mapping and Channel Selection Core engine functions: Inbound/Paid/Web, Demand Gen & ABM, Ops & Analytics, Lifecycle & Email Marketing

## **8\. Growth Marketing Strategy and Step-Change Questions**

### **Channel and Campaign Planning — Five Inputs**

Before selecting channels or planning campaigns, apply all five filters:

1. Fit with Audience and Market (from product marketing research)  
2. Fit with GTM Motion  
3. Fit with Marketing Advantages  
4. Funnel Stage Coverage (where are the gaps and leaks?)  
5. Impact / Effort (prioritization filter)

All five feed into Channel and Campaign Planning. Missing any one produces a channel strategy based on incomplete information.

### **Big Bets Principle**

20% of marketing efforts drive 80% of growth. Plan 1–2 big bets per quarter before planning everything else. Smaller tests first, then all-in. These are the step-change drivers — the initiatives that change the slope of growth, not just the volume. Everything else is pebbles and sand. Put the big rocks in first.

### **The 4 Revenue Levers — Pie Visual Model**

Expand the Pie (outer ring — New Markets): Go after a larger portion of market. Capture More of Same Pie (Current Market): Gain market share in existing market. Capture Pie More Efficiently (New Customers): Increase conversion rates and lower acquisition costs. Get More Money from Your Slice (Current Customers): Raise prices or drive expansion.

Stack rank quarterly. Get company-wide buy-in. Different lever \= different strategy, channels, and metrics.

### **Step-Change Questions by Lever**

General:

* Prioritize the 4 levers 1–4. Why that order?  
* Do you have product-market fit? Is the problem product, distribution, or market?  
* Have you done deep audience analysis and segmentation work?

Expand the Pie:

* Is there another audience you know will love the product but aren't targeting?  
* Are there unexpected use cases from free product users?  
* Is there demand from other teams at existing customer companies?  
* If you have a free product, are there people you're surprised to see using it?

Capture More of Same Pie:

* Do you have PMF with just a portion of your audience?  
* Are you seeing diminishing returns from paid search?  
* Have you signed most of your initial target accounts?

Capture Pie More Efficiently:

* Does the website tell an effective story when the right lead arrives?  
* Do landing pages exist? Do they convert higher than the homepage?  
* Is pricing clearly listed (yes, even for lower tiers)?  
* Is there friction between CTA click and product experience?  
* Can visitors book a meeting directly in the flow?  
* Have paid campaigns been optimized fully?

Get More From Existing Pie:

* Has pricing/packaging research been done?  
* Are you confident in your pricing model?  
* Has lifecycle marketing been built to nurture leads?  
* Do you get support requests for features you already have?  
* What do your most successful users have in common?  
* Which customers use the product most — has someone talked to them this quarter?

Audience and product marketing:

* What would your audience share and tell peers about?  
* Are you struggling to tell a coherent story on your homepage? Would it be easier with a more niche audience?  
* Can you get a list of every company in your addressable market?  
* Who are the top voices your audience listens to?

Adding self-serve motion (potentially):

* Do some customers close in less than a week?  
* Can you get every user to invite 3+ additional users?  
* Do people ask to buy and skip the sales process?

Adding sales-led motion (potentially):

* Do customers churn during onboarding?  
* Is this a new category requiring heavy education?  
* Are contract values increasing, requiring more hand-holding?

Market/ecosystem advantage:

* Who in the ecosystem has the most relationships with your buyers?  
* Do you integrate with products that have high branded search volume?  
* Are there rapidly developing trends requiring education or tools?

Product advantage:

* If you have network effect potential — what can marketing and product do to accelerate it? (almost always top priority)  
* Are there unused features to promote?  
* Can any product features or data become free tools?

Story/brand advantage:

* What's your best-performing content? Have all distribution and repurposing options been exhausted?  
* Does anyone in the company have a large following or strong industry opinions?  
* Is there a definitive guide for your primary audience's job to be done?

## **10\. Website Messaging Architecture**

### **The Sequencing Principle**

The template enforces a specific sequence:

1. Fill in positioning building blocks first (product type, comparator, who, what, why)  
2. Build benefits, use cases, and how-it-works blocks from those positioning inputs  
3. Write homepage copy by pulling from the blocks — never write copy directly without the blocks  
4. Do primary audience first. Secondary and tertiary audiences after. Never simultaneously.

This prevents the most common homepage failure: writing copy before positioning is clear, which produces hero sections that try to say everything to everyone and end up saying nothing to no one.

### **Messaging Building Blocks**

Top-Level Positioning Building Blocks (one per primary audience):

* What is your product type and comparator?  
* Who is your product for?  
* What is your product?  
* Why is your product better than the alternative?

Benefits Messaging Building Blocks (three benefits maximum): Per benefit: Benefit statement (value/outcome) \+ Feature or proof point 1 \+ Feature or proof point 2 \+ Feature or proof point 3

Use Case Messaging Building Blocks (three use cases maximum): Per use case: What is the use case? \+ Why is your solution better for this use case? \+ Who is this use case for?

How It Works Building Blocks: Step 1, Step 2, Step 3 — clear explanations of what happens

### **Homepage Copy Structure**

Hero Section:

* Headline: Cover problem you solve, what your product is, who it's for, and why it's better. Use top-level positioning building blocks. All four elements in the hero.  
* Value prop paragraph: Expand on headline  
* CTA button (primary action)  
* Secondary CTA link (if necessary)

Social Proof Section:

* Headline including who it's for  
* CTA (optional, linking to customer stories or case studies)

Benefits Section:

* Meta benefits headline (top-level benefit or why your product is better)  
* Benefit 1: headline \+ 3 feature/proof points \+ CTA  
* Benefit 2: same  
* Benefit 3: same

Use Cases Section:

* Section headline (focus on use cases, personas, or both depending on product and audience)  
* Use Case 1, 2, 3 (what it is, why better, who it's for)  
* CTA button

How It Works Section:

* Headline (restate what the product is)  
* Description (overview of how it solves the problem)  
* Steps 1–3  
* CTA button

Bottom Bar CTA:

* Headline (why you need the product now)  
* CTA button (Request Demo or Sign Up)  
* Secondary CTA link (if needed)

## **11\. TAM and ICP Mapping Template (Operational)**

### **Key Definitions**

Segment: A grouping or subset of your broader audience. The broad term for any division of your audience — could be by behavior, signal, size, industry, use case, or a combination.

Persona: A profile of the individual inside a company you need to reach, defined by role, jobs to be done, pain points, behavior, etc.

ICP: The combination of a company type and the key personas inside it that make the best potential buyers. MKT1 always includes both levels.

TAM: The full universe of potential customers. Not an analyst number — clarity on which company types and roles you can realistically target now and later.

### **Three-Sheet Structure**

Sheet 1: Current ICP Focus (Summary Dashboard) Quick-reference output. Captures: priority level, resource allocation % this quarter, ICP role, ICP company type, strategic notes.

Example entries:

* Core | 35% this quarter | Sales Leaders | Growth-stage B2B Startups | Add ecosystem as a channel for this audience  
* Core | 35% this quarter | Sales Leaders | Early-stage B2B Startups | Grow top of funnel  
* Scaling | 20% this quarter | Marketing Leaders | Growth-stage B2B Startups | Scale past outbound  
* Testing | 10% this quarter | Sales Leaders | Late-stage B2B | Need to redo positioning for this ICP  
* Not a priority | 0% this quarter | Sales Leaders | Public B2B | Pending results of late-stage ICP

Fill in last — after completing TAM segments and ICP prioritization sheets.

Sheet 2: TAM Segments (Universe Map) Maps all companies you could realistically go after in the next few years. Think broader than who you're targeting right now.

Per segment:

* Segment name (e.g., "B2B SaaS — Growth Stage")  
* Priority level (Core/Proven, Scaling, Testing, Not a Priority, Future Priority)  
* Industry/vertical  
* Company size range (min and max employees)  
* Geographic scope  
* Revenue range (min and max ARR)  
* Key characteristics (what makes them unique)  
* Estimated count (how many companies)

After completing: Take top-priority TAM groups and define specific roles within those company types in the ICP prioritization sheet.

Sheet 3: ICP Prioritization (Deep Definition) Takes top-priority TAM segments and builds full ICP profiles — company group plus role combination. Each ICP gets its own column.

Five categories per ICP:

1. ICP Priority and Fit:  
* Company group (from TAM sheet)  
* Role (e.g., CFOs, VP Marketing, CROs)  
* Priority level (Core / Scaling / Testing / Not a Priority)  
* Strategic rationale  
2. Goals They Need to Achieve: Jobs to be done independent of any product or solution.  
* Goal/job 1 (e.g., Share a metric in every board meeting)  
* Goal/job 2 (e.g., Send outbound emails that drive $X pipeline per month)  
* Goal/job 3  
3. Challenges in Workflow: Think beyond just the workflow your product replaces. Assess all relevant or adjacent workflows.  
* Challenge 1 (e.g., Connecting disparate tools)  
* Challenge 2 (e.g., Too much work in spreadsheets)  
* Challenge 3  
4. Other Tools They Use: Ask about their entire stack — all tools are potential partnership opportunities.  
* System of record tool (e.g., Workday or Salesforce)  
* Competitive tools (anyone you face in the sales process)  
* Complementary tools (integrations or other products used alongside yours)  
* Homegrown templates, utilities, tools (great for content ideas)  
5. Behavior and Content Consumption: Most often skipped in customer interviews — but critical for channel and content strategy.  
* Where they find information (communities, peers, search, publications, platforms, key influencers)  
* How they typically buy products (self-serve, sales, channel/vendor, through IT, hybrid)  
* How they like to be communicated with (design-oriented, brand and voice important, bullet points, long-form content)  
6. Primary Use Cases in Your Product:  
* Use case 1 (e.g., Dashboarding)  
* Use case 2 (e.g., Event planning)  
* Use case 3

### **Process Sequence**

1. Map all potential TAM segments in Sheet 2 — think broader than current targets  
2. Assign priority levels to each segment  
3. Take top-priority segments to Sheet 3 and define specific role \+ company type combinations  
4. Fill in goals, challenges, tools, behavior, and use cases per ICP through customer interviews and research  
5. Summarize top-priority ICPs in Sheet 1 with resource allocation percentages  
6. Feed Sheet 1 summary into Marketing Decision Dashboard  
7. Use ICP definitions to inform positioning, content roadmap, GACCS Briefs, and channel selection

## **12\. Positioning Mistakes, Diagnostics, and Pocus Teardown**

### **Five Positioning Mistakes**

1. Bad Research: Jump straight to positioning without understanding audience and market. If you haven't done your research, you'll spin your wheels and won't end up in the right place.  
2. Can't See the Forest Through the Trees: Spend so much time on the process that you lose sight of the differentiator. Getting so deep in the research that you can't synthesize it into a clear, simple statement.  
3. Kitchen Sink Approach: Go way too broad — trying to include everything — and end up saying nothing to no one. Too many features, too many benefits, too many differentiators. Pick one.  
4. Inside Baseball: The team knows the product so well they forget the audience doesn't. Positioning written in internal language, for internal comfort, not for external clarity.  
5. Game of Telephone: The positioning itself is clear, but the copy misses the mark. The translation from positioning to messaging to copy breaks down — typically because the writer didn't understand the positioning deeply enough or forgot what funnel stage and ICP they were writing for.

Root causes: Too many cooks. Fear of picking the wrong direction. Confusion from too many audience segments. Forgetting what funnel stage, channel, or ICP you're writing for.

### **Brand Story → Positioning → Messaging Hierarchy**

Brand Story (left layer):

* Perceptions (aka storylines)  
* Company mission, vision, story  
* Product story

Positioning (middle layer):

* Positioning Product 1  
* Positioning Product 2  
* Positioning Product 3 Product story feeds into each one.

Messaging (right layer):

* By audience  
* By funnel stage  
* By channel

Sequence: Brand story informs positioning. Positioning informs messaging. Messaging informs copywriting. Never in reverse.

### **Pocus Teardown — Universal Diagnostic Principles**

This teardown (joint work by Emily Kramer/MKT1 and Anthony Pierri/Fletch) shows how to diagnose and fix a positioning problem in practice.

Current Pocus positioning (as submitted):

* Product type: New way / Comparator: Combo of multiple workflows  
* Who: Sales & GTM teams (Customer Success, RevOps, Marketing)  
* What: Platform to run GTM playbooks, powered by AI  
* Why: Build more pipeline and close more revenue  
* Homepage hero: "Turn (any) signal into revenue"

Suggested positioning (Emily \+ Anthony):

* Product type: New way (confirmed correct) / Comparator: Combo of multiple workflows (confirmed correct)  
* Who: Sales teams with multiple reps (primary). Other GTM teams \= secondary audience.  
* What: Pocus combines prospecting data (AI prospecting, intent signals, product data) into proven playbooks with AI  
* Why Option A: Stop wasting time researching and reaching out to the wrong prospects  
* Why Option B: Increase revenue with streamlined prospecting  
* Homepage hero: "All the data reps need to reach the right prospects, at the right time"

Audience segmentation for positioning (only one drives homepage):

| Segment | Definition | Priority | Focus for Positioning |
| ----- | ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Segment 1 | Sales teams at SaaS companies (target revenue range) | 1 | Yes |
| Segment 2 | RevOps teams at SaaS companies | 2 | No |
| Segment 3 | CS teams at SaaS companies | 2 | No |
| Segment 4 | Marketing teams at SaaS companies | 3 | No |

Universal diagnostic principles extracted from this teardown:

1. Position against the old way until the category matures: Early-stage new way products should not waste homepage positioning on emerging competitors. The majority of potential customers aren't using any solution in the category yet. Win them first. Compete later. As the category matures, shift to 10x better positioning.  
2. One landing use case, not a feature list: The homepage hero needs a single use case as the entry point for the primary audience. Additional use cases live in sub-pages.  
3. One primary audience per homepage: Pick the highest-priority segment. Write the hero for them. Route all other segments to their own pages. If you can't pick one, the audience definition work isn't done.  
4. "Build more pipeline and close more revenue" is not a differentiator: Every sales tool makes this claim. It's the category-level outcome, not a specific benefit.  
5. Language sophistication must match audience sophistication: Modern SaaS org language (product-led sales, intent signals, warm prospecting) is meaningless to legacy buyers. Legacy-level language undersells to modern buyers. Know which you're writing for before choosing vocabulary.  
6. Positioning statement ≠ homepage hero copy: The positioning statement is the strategic input. The homepage hero is the execution. The hero must include all three parts (who, what, why) but written in the language and awareness level of the target audience.

The guitar teacher analogy: Telling a beginner guitarist you'll teach them "hybrid picking, artificial harmonics, polyrhythms, and metric modulation" scares them off. "I'll teach you how to play Wonderwall" converts them. Don't introduce five new concepts when the audience is barely ready for one. This applies directly to new way positioning.
