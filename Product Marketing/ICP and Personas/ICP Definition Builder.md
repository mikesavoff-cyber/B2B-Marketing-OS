# **ICP Definition Builder**

## **Purpose**

This skill produces a full-stack ICP: one that defines not just *who buys*, but: 0\. Whether the segment is actually confirmed by usage, retention, and enthusiasm data — or still a hypothesis (PostHog data validation)

1. How proven each segment is (MKT1 maturity)  
2. What energy state they're in relative to the problem (FletchPMM M1/M2/M3)  
3. Whether they'll activate, retain, and expand in a PLG motion (Wes Bush MOAT)  
4. Who the buyer, user, champion, and economic decision-maker are (buyer architecture)

The output feeds every downstream PMM deliverable. No messaging, positioning, or copy gets written without an ICP profile as input.

---

## **Framework Architecture**

Four frameworks run in sequence, each adding a layer:

**PostHog Data Validation** → the ground truth layer: every claim below it must be checked against real usage, retention, and enthusiasm signals — not assumed **MKT1 ICP Prioritization** → structural backbone: segment definition, maturity, time allocation **FletchPMM M1/M2/M3** → energy layer: narrative strategy, villain, proof format **Wes Bush MOAT** → PLG fit layer: activation, adoption, touch-to-value, expansion dynamics

Customer data is the most important context source of all. An ICP built from a founder's best guess is a hypothesis. An ICP built from retention, usage, and enthusiasm data is a fact. Everything downstream — positioning, messaging, launch briefs — is only as strong as the data layer underneath it.

---

## **Step 0 — Data Validation Layer (PostHog Method)**

Run this before Step 1, and re-run it any time a segment gets promoted or demoted in maturity. This is not a one-time exercise — it's the ongoing check that keeps every other layer honest.

### **The Core Rule**

A broad ICP is worse than no ICP. "B2B startups" or "mid-market companies" aren't ICPs — they're industries. If a segment definition can't be tested and falsified, it's not doing any work.

### **ICP vs. Persona — Keep These Separate**

* **ICP**: the company or team. Firmographics, size, stage, what they believe, what they're trying to do.  
* **Persona**: the individual — job title, day-to-day motivations, what they personally need from the product.

MKT1's Step 1 (Role \+ Company Type) collapses these into one line. That's fine for a fast segment map, but when a segment gets promoted to Core, split it back out — define the ICP (team-level) and the Persona(s) inside it (role-level) separately. A team can be squarely in the ICP while containing three different personas with three different messages.

### **Start Narrow, Add Detail as Confidence Builds**

Don't wait for a complete profile before testing. The correct sequence:

1. Best guess with 3 attributes, tested immediately against real signups/customers  
2. Gather intel continuously — signup questions, retention comparisons, power-user analysis, NPS/PMF survey responses  
3. Add detail only as data confirms it — each added attribute should narrow the definition, not just describe it further  
4. You'll know the ICP is right when it feels almost too narrow. That discomfort is the signal, not a problem to fix.

### **The Data Signals That Confirm an ICP**

A segment is validated when it outperforms the rest of the base on:

* **Retention**: retains better than non-ICP users  
* **Usage**: uses the product more, and triggers high-value events faster or more often  
* **Survey signal**: rates higher on NPS/PMF surveys than non-ICP users  
* **Sales cycle**: closes faster than average

If a segment looks right on paper but doesn't show up in these signals, the segment is wrong — not the data.

### **Real Enthusiasm vs. Polite Indifference**

Founders get lied to gently. Learn to tell the difference.

**Real enthusiasm (ICP signal)**:

* Visibly excited in the demo, not just polite  
* Strongly agrees with the problem framing, unprompted  
* Gives ongoing, specific feedback  
* Invites colleagues without being asked  
* Uses the product even when it's rough  
* Refers other people with the same problem  
* Starts paying, or asks to pay before you're charging  
* Shares the product publicly

**Polite indifference (false positive)**:

* Calls it "interesting," "cool," or "clever" — praise with no commitment behind it  
* Uses it for something you didn't intend (worth investigating, not celebrating)  
* Says they wouldn't pay for it when asked directly  
* Refers you to someone junior with no purchase authority

### **Timing and Patience**

* Start once there's a working product and some initial traction. Waiting too long costs more time than starting rough.  
* A real ICP takes 3 months to a year to sharpen. It will keep evolving — don't let it go stale, but don't panic if it's not final on day one.  
* Don't lock in off 1–2 successful customers. 5 similar paying customers is a real signal for B2B; 10+ is strong confirmation. Anything less might be an outlier dragging you off course.  
* Cold outbound is a legitimate ICP-testing tool, not just a growth channel — a cold response is more honest than a warm intro. Warm intros produce polite yeses that look like signal and aren't.

### **Data Validation Checklist (run before promoting a segment's maturity)**

*  Does this segment retain better than the rest of the base?  
*  Does this segment use the product more, and reach value faster?  
*  Does this segment rate higher on NPS/PMF surveys?  
*  Do we have 5+ paying customers who look like this (10+ for high confidence)?  
*  Have we seen real enthusiasm signals, not just polite interest?  
*  Is the definition narrow enough that it could be wrong — or is it still an industry label wearing an ICP costume?

If any box is unchecked, the segment stays at its current maturity level. Don't promote on vibes.

## **Step 1 — Segment Definition and Maturity (MKT1 Framework)**

### **How to Define a Segment**

Every segment is defined by two things only:

* **Role**: The buyer or decision-maker. Specific. "VP of Marketing" not "marketers." "Head of RevOps" not "ops teams."  
* **Company Type**: Industry, size, stage, and defining characteristics specific enough to build a target list from. "Series B SaaS, 100–500 employees, product-led motion" not "tech companies."

Role \+ Company Type \= a segment specific enough to target.

### **The Four Maturity Levels**

Maturity is based on evidence, not aspiration. This is the most common mistake: calling something Core because you *want* it to be.

| Level | Name | What it means |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| **1** | **Core** | PMF proven. Shortest sales cycle, highest win rate, most efficient acquisition. You've closed many of these. You know them. |
| **2** | **Scaling** | Adjacent segment you're actively expanding into. Product works for them, but messaging, channel, or sales motion may still need refinement. Not yet as efficient as Core. |
| **3** | **Testing** | Early signal, unproven. A few customers, some inbound, or a product bet. No repeatable success yet. |
| **4** | **Future** | Intentionally deprioritized. Product not ready, market not ready, or insufficient resources. Naming it here prevents it from sneaking into plans. |

**Rules for assigning maturity:**

* Most companies have 1–2 Core segments. If you have 4+, push harder: which has the strongest evidence — shortest cycle, highest win rate?  
* If there's no Future segment, something is missing. Every company has audiences they should NOT be actively pursuing right now.  
* If two segments have the same buyer, same sales cycle, and same messaging — they're one segment. Collapse them.

### **Time Allocation Today**

What percentage of marketing's time and budget is *actually* going to each segment right now? Not what you wish it were — what it is.

Numbers should add up to \~100%. Directional is fine.

**What time allocation reveals:**

* Spending 40% on a Testing segment because one exec is excited about it  
* Spending nothing on Core because "it runs itself"  
* Spreading time evenly across segments instead of concentrating on the proven ones

When time allocation doesn't match maturity, call it out directly. That's a strategy-execution mismatch.

### **MKT1 ICP Priority Map (Output Format)**

\#\# ICPs BY MATURITY

| Role | Company Type | Description | Maturity | Time Allocation Today |  
|------|-------------|-------------|----------|-----------------------|  
| \[Specific buyer role\] | \[Industry, size, stage\] | \[1-2 sentences: what makes this segment distinct — buying behavior, use case, problem\] | 1 — Core | \~X% |  
| \[Role\] | \[Company type\] | \[Description\] | 2 — Scaling | \~X% |  
| \[Role\] | \[Company type\] | \[Description\] | 3 — Testing | \~X% |  
| \[Role\] | \[Company type\] | \[Description\] | 4 — Future | 0% |

## **Step 2 — Market Energy Layer (FletchPMM M1/M2/M3)**

For each segment in the MKT1 priority map, classify the energy state. This determines the entire narrative logic for that segment before a word of messaging is written.

> Don't start with firmographics. Start with energy. The M-type determines what you say, not just who you say it to.

### **M1 — Potential Energy**

The desire exists. Action is blocked.

* They *want* the outcome your product enables but aren't actively pursuing it  
* Barriers in the way: cost, time, complexity, lack of awareness  
* They haven't normalized a workaround — they've normalized the *absence* of a solution

**Narrative logic**: Remove the barrier. Prove the outcome is now reachable. Don't compare to alternatives — there's nothing to compare against. **Villain**: The barrier itself (cost, complexity, friction) **Proof format**: Before/after — what's now possible that wasn't before

**Duolingo example**: 80% of users weren't trying to learn a language before signing up. They wanted to. Tutors were too expensive. Commute time was wasted. Duolingo removed both barriers.

---

### **M2 — Kinetic Energy**

The activity is already happening — worse.

* They're already doing the thing your product addresses, but manually, expensively, or badly  
* Spreadsheets instead of software. Email threads instead of tools. Copy-paste instead of automation  
* The workaround has become normalized ("that's just how we do it here")

**Narrative logic**: Redirect existing energy. Name the workaround specifically. Show the same outcome with less friction.**Villain**: The workaround itself — name it concretely, not generically **Proof format**: Effort and time comparison — what they do today vs. what they do with you

**DocuSign example**: People were sharing and signing documents — on paper. Same outcome, simpler path.

**Note**: M2 in a red ocean is the highest-leverage PLG ICP combination. Buyer already has the workflow, already knows the category, just needs to experience your product being better.

---

### **M3 — Captured Energy**

Already using a competitor. Category-mature.

* They've evaluated the space, made a choice, and chose someone else  
* They know the players, the pricing models, the standard capabilities  
* You're competing for existing spend, not creating new behavior

**Narrative logic**: Differentiation is the whole game. Find the frustrations they already have with their current tool and name them. **Villain**: Incumbent limitations — specifically the things your M3 target already resents **Proof format**: Head-to-head — specific capability comparison on the dimensions they actually care about

**Figma example**: Launched directly at Adobe users. No category education needed. Just: you already use these. Ours is better.

---

### **Message-Segment Mismatch Warning**

Wrong message \+ right segment \= wrong message. This is where GTM budget disappears.

Telling an M3 user they'll "finally be able to track leads" means they think you're clueless — they've been tracking leads for years, in a powerful tool. Telling an M1 user you have an "enjoyable CRM" means nothing to someone who doesn't know what a CRM is.

For each segment in the MKT1 map, record:

* M-type  
* Current behavior (what they do instead of using your product)  
* Trigger event (what changes that makes them open to switching)  
* Workaround (the specific thing they do today that your product replaces)

## **Step 3 — PLG Fit Layer (Wes Bush MOAT Framework)**

For each segment, run all four MOAT dimensions. Each gets a flag: 🟢 Green / 🟡 Yellow / 🔴 Red.

See `distilled/Wes Bush - MOAT PLG Fit.md` for full flag definitions, scoring rubrics, and compound flag warnings.

### **M — Market Strategy**

Does this segment's buying behavior support PLG?

* SMB → 🟢 Full self-serve appropriate  
* Mid-market → 🟡 Hybrid PLG; product-led sales model  
* Enterprise → 🔴 PLG supports but doesn't lead; sales-led primary

**ICP question**: Does this buyer expect to try before committing, or do they require a demo, proposal, and procurement cycle?

---

### **O — Ocean Conditions**

Do they already understand the category, or do they need education first?

* Red ocean (aware buyers) → 🟢 PLG widens the funnel cheaply; buyers know what they're signing up for  
* Blue ocean (unaware buyers) → 🟡 Education must precede product experience; lead with content/marketing before PLG

**ICP question**: If we gave this buyer a free trial today, would they know what to do with it — or would they need a webinar first?

**Interaction warning**: Yellow O \+ Yellow A (adoption) stacked means you're asking users to educate themselves on a new category *and* develop new skills simultaneously. That's very hard to scale. Flag it explicitly.

---

### **A — Adoption**

How many hurdles does this user need to clear before experiencing value?

| Layer | Description | Flag |
| ----- | ----- | ----- |
| Product adoption only | Navigate and complete a core action without help. Zoom, Slack, Notion. | 🟢 |
| \+ Knowledge adoption | Must understand something new (not just how to click, but what to know to get results). Salesforce requires sales process knowledge. | 🟡 |
| \+ Skill adoption | Must develop a new capability. "Learn where to click" ≠ "learn how to think differently." | 🔴 |

**Founder blind spot warning**: Founders systematically underestimate adoption friction because they have years of domain context their users don't. Test with someone who has never seen the product.

---

### **T — Touch-to-Value**

How much of *your organization's* involvement is required before the user experiences value?

* No-touch (Canva, Grammarly) → 🟢 Widest PLG funnel  
* Low-touch (Slack — self-serve for small teams, sales for enterprise deals) → 🟡 Automate more over time  
* High-touch (CSM/implementation required before value) → 🔴 Not disqualifying; early high-touch is a research strategy you deliberately reduce

**ICP question**: If we removed every human from onboarding, what % of this segment would still reach activation?

---

### **MOAT Score per Segment**

| Score | Implication |
| ----- | ----- |
| All 🟢 | PLG-native. Full self-serve motion. |
| 1–2 🟡 | PLG works. Address yellows intentionally. |
| 🟡 stacking (3+) | Build the foundation before the motion. Fix flags first. |
| 🔴 on M (pure enterprise) | PLG supports but doesn't lead. |

## **Step 4 — Buyer Architecture**

For each prioritized segment, map the full buying system. In B2B PLG — especially mid-market and hybrid motions — the user and the buyer are rarely the same person.

See `distilled/Buyer Architecture & PQL.md` for full templates. Key fields:

**End User**: Role, job-to-be-done, activation definition (what action \= value reached), first-week success criteria, champion signal behaviors

**Economic Buyer**: Role, buying trigger, primary risk concerns, proof requirements, deal size threshold for involvement

**Champion Profile**: Who turns from user to internal advocate, what they need to sell internally (ROI framing, security docs, case studies), their risk exposure if the purchase goes wrong

**Expansion Dynamics**: Bottom-up (individual → team → org) vs. top-down; value multiplier type (collaboration, data, network, none); natural expansion trigger

**PQL Threshold Candidates**: 2–3 behavioral signals that indicate a user has experienced enough value to be sales-ready. Find these in data, not theory. Slack's example: 2,000 messages sent. That threshold was discovered empirically — users who hit it almost never churned.

## **How This Profile Feeds Downstream PMM Work**

**Before messaging or copy**: Load the ICP profile. The `Lead message`, `Villain`, and `Proof format` fields are the inputs.

**Before positioning**: M-type \+ MOAT determines narrative strategy. M2 \+ Red ocean \= villain-heavy (workaround as villain). M1 \+ Blue ocean \= change-and-stakes narrative. M3 \= differentiation-heavy.

**Before competitive briefs**: The M3 segment and maturity level drive competitive depth. Weight each competitor section by how often they appear in *this segment's* actual evaluation process. Not evenly. Not alphabetically.

**Before launch briefs**: The buyer architecture (end user / economic buyer / champion) determines the multi-threaded launch narrative.

**Before battle cards**: The MOAT score and competitive framing field determine the win narrative for each segment.

---

## **Writing Quality Check**

All ICP-derived output must pass this check before delivery:

* **No triple value prop**: The `Lead message` field is the hierarchy. One thing leads. Subordinate the rest.  
* **No hedged recommendations**: MOAT assessments and maturity scores are recommendations. State them directly.  
* **No throat-clearing**: Any document derived from this profile opens with the recommendation, not the context.  
* **No mirror-image competitor sections**: Weight by competitive intensity, not equal treatment.  
* **No binary contrasts**: The `Current behavior` and `Workaround` fields give you the specificity. Use it. Not "we're not just another CRM" — but "your team exports from Salesforce, Gainsight, and Intercom into a Friday afternoon spreadsheet."
