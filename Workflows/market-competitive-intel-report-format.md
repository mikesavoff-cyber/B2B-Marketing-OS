# Market & Competitive Intelligence — Report Format

This is the output contract for `market-competitive-intel-research.md` Step 3 (and everything downstream that Step 3 feeds). It replaces the earlier, thinner 7-point brief structure with the full deliverable stack below. Referenced from Step 3, not inlined there — this file is the depth, Step 3 stays the pointer.

**What this is not:** a research dump, a generic SWOT, or a giant competitor spreadsheet. The bar: it should feel like a senior PMM took messy market evidence, formed a point of view, and made the implications obvious for leadership, product, marketing, and sales — not like a compliance audit of everything that was found.

**Core principle:** a small decision document, supported by a deeper evidence base, plus a set of reusable operating artifacts. The polished executive layer is not the whole deliverable — it's the decision layer. The report and evidence base make the conclusions credible; the operational assets make them useful after anyone stops reading.

## The deliverable stack — five connected layers

| Layer | Primary job | Typical format | Primary reader |
|---|---|---|---|
| Executive decision artifact | State what matters and what to do | 8-15-slide deck or 2-4-page brief | Founder, leadership, GTM team |
| Analytical report | Show the reasoning and evidence | 15-35-page document | PMM, product, strategy |
| Reusable intelligence assets | Make findings usable in daily work | Sheets, Notion database, tables | PMM, product, sales |
| Visual models | Make market structure and strategic gaps visible | Maps, matrices, charts | Everyone |
| Action handoff | Turn findings into decisions and work | Prioritized recommendations, briefs, tickets | Product, marketing, sales |

---

## Layer 1 — Executive decision artifact

Answers three questions almost immediately: what's changing in the market, how do we currently compare, what should we do next.

**Decision headline** (not a topic label). Not "Competitive Intelligence Report: [Category]." Instead, the actual conclusion stated as a sentence — e.g. "The market is converging around AI-assisted planning, but competitors still force buyers to choose between fast adoption and operational depth. Our clearest opportunity is to own implementation confidence for scaling teams."

**Executive verdict** — three to five concise verdicts, each traceable to evidence elsewhere in the report: Market reality, Buyer reality, Competitive reality (who wins on what), Our reality (our actual advantage vs. whether our messaging makes it legible), Strategic implication.

**The important shifts** — only the shifts that could change a decision, not an inventory of everything that happened: a competitor moving upmarket, a free tier resetting category expectations, categories converging, buyer evaluation criteria changing, a competitor gaining authority through integrations/proof/distribution, a gap between perceived and actual strength. Prioritize change and consequence over completeness.

**Competitive structure** — who competes for the same buyer and use case; which competitors are direct, adjacent, substitute, or aspirational; how each one wins; where the company has a meaningful right to win; which apparent "gaps" are strategically irrelevant and not worth chasing.

**Recommended actions** — a short prioritized table, specific enough to become a brief, a roadmap input, a sales-enablement task, or an experiment:

| Priority | Recommendation | Owner | Why now | Expected effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|

## Layer 2 — The core analytical report

Reads as an argument: here's the decision context, here's what the evidence shows, here's what it means, here's what to do.

1. **Mandate and decision context** — why this research was commissioned, what decision it supports, scope (market/segment/product/geography), research date, in/out of scope, the central questions being answered. "This assessment evaluates whether the company should continue positioning as X or narrow toward Y" — not "this report analyzes the competitive landscape."

2. **Executive verdict** — 1-2 pages max, readable standalone without the rest of the report: overall conclusion, 3-6 major findings, strategic implications, recommended actions, confidence level, critical unknowns.

3. **Market reality** — how the market actually works, not just a category definition. Cover: category boundaries, maturity, key segments, buyer jobs-to-be-done and evaluation criteria, trigger events that create demand, budget ownership, substitutes and internal alternatives, emerging category language, structural forces. Example of the right altitude: "Buyers may describe the problem as 'better reporting,' but the commercial trigger is often a loss of control during organizational growth" — that distinction matters more than a market-size paragraph.

4. **Buyer and ICP reality** — primary ICP, subsegments, economic buyer, users/influencers, trigger events, current alternatives, selection criteria, switching barriers, reasons for choosing or rejecting each competitor, the actual language buyers use. Don't stop at firmographics — this section earns its place by explaining *why* the buyer changes, evaluates, chooses, rejects, or switches.

5. **Company reality** — the target's own actual product strengths, perceived strengths, proof available to support them, weaknesses that matter in active deals, capabilities not yet differentiated, distribution/brand advantages, pricing/packaging position, adoption/retention constraints where evidence exists, claims the company can credibly own. The real question isn't "what features do we have" — it's "which advantages are meaningful to the target buyer, defensible against alternatives, and actually visible in the buying process."

6. **Competitive landscape** — separate competitors by strategic role: direct, adjacent, substitute, internal build/manual process, emerging entrant, category leader that shapes buyer expectations. Don't treat every company as equally important — the most relevant "competitor" is sometimes a spreadsheet or an internal ops team, not the company with the most similar feature list.

7. **Competitor profiles** — one page each, analytical not encyclopedic: company/product, strategic role, target customer, core use case, primary positioning, category language, main proof points, product strengths/weaknesses, pricing/packaging signals, GTM motion, distribution advantages, customer sentiment, why buyers choose them, why buyers reject or leave them, threat level, implication for us. End each with an interpretive sentence, e.g. "Competitor A is not strongest because it has the broadest product. It is strongest because it makes adoption feel low-risk for teams with limited operational maturity." That's the level of interpretation to aim for — not a features list.

## Layer 3 — Reusable intelligence assets

Artifacts that stay useful after the research project ends, not one-time report content.

**Evidence ledger** — the source-of-truth table. Fields: claim, evidence type, source, source date, direct quote/observed fact, interpretation, confidence, contradicting evidence, strategic relevance, last verified date. Keep fact and interpretation in separate columns, always — e.g. "Competitor A introduced a lower-priced entry plan" (observation) vs. "may be defending against freemium entrants" (interpretation). This separation is what stops plausible speculation from being presented as fact.

**Competitive matrix** — compares dimensions that actually affect buying decisions, not every feature. Rows: target segment, core JTBD, primary use case, positioning, implementation model, time to value, key workflow, integrations, governance, reporting, AI capabilities, pricing model, entry point, proof strength, main weakness, best-fit customer, competitive threat. Use a small, honest scale (Strong / Adequate / Limited / Unknown) — never a 1-10 score unless the scoring method is genuinely defensible.

**Positioning map** — visualizes a real strategic tension, not decorative axes. Good axis pairs: self-serve adoption ↔ guided implementation, point solution ↔ operating platform, tactical automation ↔ strategic orchestration, SMB simplicity ↔ enterprise governance, breadth of use cases ↔ depth in one critical workflow. Every axis needs its own justification (why it matters to buyers, what evidence supports the placement) and an explicit label of whether it's mapping observed positioning, actual product capability, buyer perception, or some blend — those are different maps and must not be conflated.

**Pricing and packaging view** — pricing model, entry price/signal, free plan or trial, packaging logic, usage/seat mechanics, enterprise gating, discount signals, what customers appear to actually pay for, how pricing reinforces positioning. Every price point gets a confidence label: Public price / Observed quote / Customer-reported range / Inferred signal / Unknown. Never present an estimate as a fact.

**Message comparison** — verbatim language, side by side, one column per company: homepage headline, category label, problem led with, buyer named, main outcome, proof used, objection pre-empted, call to action. This is what separates product differentiation from message differentiation — two products can be genuinely different while sounding identical, or genuinely similar while sounding different.

**Buyer language bank** — verbatim language from reviews, customer interviews, communities, sales calls, win/loss interviews, support discussions, analyst commentary. Organized by: desired outcome, pain, trigger, objection, competitor praise, competitor criticism, switching reason, trust signal. Direct input to positioning, messaging, content, sales enablement, and demand gen work.

## Layer 4 — Visual models

The positioning map above is the primary one. General rule for any visual in this system: one visual idea per chart, always labeled with what kind of claim it's making (observed fact vs. perception vs. capability), never decorative. A chart with no decision attached to it doesn't belong in the deliverable.

## Layer 5 — Action handoff (operational outputs)

**Sales battlecards** — one to two pages, answering: when does this competitor appear, why do buyers choose them, where are they genuinely strong, where are they vulnerable, what should the seller ask, what should the seller avoid claiming, how should we position against them, what proof should we use, what objection response is appropriate, when should we walk away rather than force the deal. Not "we are better because we are more flexible" — instead: "If the buyer prioritizes rapid self-serve setup, Competitor A may be the better fit. If they need cross-functional governance at scale, ask how they'll handle that as the team grows. Use the implementation case study to show our depth." This is the upgrade to the existing story-arc battlecard format (`market-competitive-intel-research.md` Step 5) — the story arc is *how* it gets delivered live on a call; this is *what* it has to actually contain to be credible, not just dramatic.

**Positioning handoff** — best-fit market frame, target buyer, high-value problem, relevant alternative, differentiated value, reasons to believe, proof gaps, claims to avoid, potential positioning territories, messaging hypotheses that still need validation. Explicitly does not claim competitive research alone proves the final positioning — it identifies the strongest territories and names what still needs testing. Hands off to the separate `pmm-positioning` skill, doesn't try to finish that job itself.

**Product and roadmap inputs** — capability gaps, proof gaps, packaging opportunities, integration priorities, onboarding improvements, trust/governance requirements, where competitors are over-serving the market, where feature parity wouldn't create strategic value. Every recommendation states which kind of problem it actually is: product requirement, proof/case-study requirement, messaging problem, sales-enablement problem, pricing/packaging problem, or an evidence gap needing more research. This typing matters — a "we need more proof" finding and a "we need to build this feature" finding look similar as bullet points but require completely different owners.

**Monitoring system** — competitor watchlist, change log, pricing-change tracker, product-launch tracker, message-change tracker, monthly/quarterly digest, alert thresholds, owner and review cadence. This is what `Workflows/battlecard-weekly-refresh.md` already implements — cross-reference it here rather than rebuilding it. A recurring digest never repeats full profiles; it only answers what changed, why it might matter, and what to do about it.

## What it should look and feel like

**Visually:** editorial, not bureaucratic. Calm, not overloaded. Evidence-led, not decorative. Structured but not sterile. Commercially aware, not academic. Specific enough to act on. Use: strong page hierarchy, short paragraphs, clear takeaway headlines, small readable tables, consistent competitor colors, evidence labels, callout boxes for implications, one visual idea per slide, real whitespace, source notes that don't overwhelm the main message.

**Avoid:** giant feature matrices with no interpretation, decorative 2×2 maps, repeated logos, excessive color coding, dense unbroken paragraphs, generic SWOT grids, unexplained scores, screenshots standing in for analysis, charts with no decision attached.

**In writing:** direct, precise, candid, analytical, commercially literate, comfortable stating uncertainty. "Competitor B has stronger enterprise proof, but its complexity creates an adoption burden for the lower end of the segment" — not "Competitor B has many strengths and weaknesses." "Our product appears differentiated at the capability level, but that differentiation isn't visible in the current category narrative" — not "there is an opportunity to differentiate." Avoid technically-safe, strategically-empty sentences ("the market is competitive and rapidly evolving") — if a sentence would still be true for any company in any category, cut it.

**In rhythm:** every section moves through the same five beats — observation, interpretation, implication, recommendation, unknown. Worked example:

> **Observation:** Three major competitors now lead with AI-assisted workflow automation.
> **Interpretation:** "AI-powered" is becoming category hygiene rather than differentiation.
> **Implication:** Repeating the same claim will not improve distinctiveness.
> **Recommendation:** Use AI as a supporting mechanism and lead with the operational outcome it enables.
> **Unknown:** It is not yet clear whether buyers value AI itself or simply faster completion of the underlying workflow.

## The ideal final package

For a B2B SaaS target, the default package is:

1. **Executive deck** — 10-15 slides: verdicts, market shifts, competitive structure, decisions.
2. **Core report** — 20-30 pages, full analytical narrative (Layer 2).
3. **Evidence ledger** — facts, interpretations, confidence, sources, separated.
4. **Competitive matrix** — decision-relevant comparison across the target and 3-5 meaningful competitors (matches Step 1's tiered cap).
5. **Positioning map** — one or two evidence-backed visuals showing real strategic whitespace or buyer tension.
6. **Competitor profiles** — one to two pages per priority (Tier 1) competitor.
7. **Pricing and packaging view** — with confidence labels throughout.
8. **Battlecards** — one page per competitor that actually appears in real deals.
9. **Positioning handoff** — territories, proof points, claims to avoid, unresolved hypotheses.
10. **Action register** — prioritized marketing/product/sales/research actions with owners.
11. **Monitoring artifact** — the change log and recurring digest (already built as `battlecard-weekly-refresh.md`).

**The governing design principle:** the executive layer should be short enough to use in a leadership meeting, the report should be rigorous enough to audit, and the operational artifacts should be useful enough to actually change daily GTM behavior. That's what separates this from "competitor research."
